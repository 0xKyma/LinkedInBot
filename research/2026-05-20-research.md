### Candidate 1 — US Navy Shipbuilding Plan: Doubled Budget, No More Ships
**Source:** USNI News, National Law Review, Navy Times, Breaking Defense
**URLs:** https://news.usni.org/2026/05/11/u-s-navys-2026-shipbuilding-plan | https://natlawreview.com/article/navy-shipbuilding-plan-signals-industrial-shift
**Date:** 11–12 May 2026


The Department of the Navy currently operates 291 battle force ships against a statutory requirement of 355, and despite a doubling of the shipbuilding budget over two decades, fleet size has not meaningfully increased. The plan describes this not merely as an industrial problem — it is structural, and "the result of how we buy, how we plan, and how we manage risk in Navy acquisition."



The Navy's acquisition system grew administratively but eroded operationally — requirements expanded during execution, mature designs were modified mid-programme, and cost and schedule estimates were chronically optimistic. As a result, schedules slipped, costs compounded, and the American taxpayer absorbed the bill.


As a partial response, 
the Navy plans to increase reliance on distributed shipbuilding sites, aiming to grow from 10% of shipbuilding at distributed sites today to 50%, to increase flexibility and accelerate delivery.


**SE/Systems Thinking Angle:** This is a textbook **verification gap** and **requirements volatility** case study at the largest scale imaginable. The system (acquisition) performed exactly as designed — it processed requirements, contracts, and budget — but the design was wrong for the actual operational need (fleet size). The Navy's own plan states that requirements expanded *during execution* and that designs were modified after programme start. This is the requirements creep / unstable baseline failure mode that systems engineers fight daily, now playing out across a 30-year, multi-hundred-billion-dollar programme. The move toward modular, distributed shipbuilding is also a direct systems architecture play — an attempt to shift from monolithic, single-yard builds to a loosely coupled production system-of-systems.

The non-obvious angle for an SE audience: this is what happens when a system's operational concept is defined against a threat model that's smaller than the actual threat — a verification gap at the energy-security system level.
