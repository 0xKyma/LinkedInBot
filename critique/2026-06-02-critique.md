## SysML v1 vs SysML v2: What Changes for Engineering Teams? — SodiusWillert

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

The move to SysML v2 is a metamodel replacement, and most teams are treating it like a tooling upgrade.

KerML breaks the UML inheritance that underpinned v1. V2 unifies behavioural semantics across activities, states, and interactions in a way v1 never managed. The model-diagram separation means diagrams become views, not the source of truth. For teams who built their practice around block diagrams, that is a significant shift.

The versioning story is genuinely new. V2 standardises commits and branches inside the API spec, so version control becomes a language-level concern rather than a tool-vendor workaround.

The gap between understanding this transition conceptually and being operationally ready for it is wide on most programmes I've reviewed. Read that checklist from Eran Gery before your team starts a v2 pilot.

Source: https://www.sodiuswillert.com/en/blog/sysml-v1-vs-sysml-v2-what-changes-for-engineering-teams

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (The displacement)

SysML v2 doesn't extend v1. It replaces the conceptual foundation underneath it.

That distinction matters for the tooling market. Every vendor who built on UML-based v1 semantics now carries technical debt at the metamodel level. The move to KerML as the common semantic base creates a genuine displacement event, and the REST API plus JSON interchange mechanism signals where the spec authors expect value to accumulate: in model exchange and integration, not in any single authoring tool.

That's a meaningful bet. It implies the competitive advantage in MBSE tooling shifts from modelling ergonomics toward interoperability and ecosystem depth. Teams evaluating toolchains in 2026 should be asking vendors about their KerML conformance and API roadmap, not just their diagram palette.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Teams worried about breaking their v1 models in a v2 transition should ask why those models are hard to migrate.

In most cases the answer is the same: diagram-as-model conflation, loose behavioural semantics, tool-specific versioning hacks. These are existing gaps that v2 makes visible. The transition didn't create them. The difficulty of migration is diagnostic.

The SodiusWillert breakdown by Eran Gery is technically solid. Read it as a mirror, not just a roadmap. For many programmes, the v1 baseline someone wants to migrate was never as rigorous as it appeared from the outside.

V2 readiness is partly a tooling question, and partly a question of whether your current modelling practice can survive a proper semantic audit.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown

Most teams scoping a SysML v2 transition focus on what's new. The harder work is figuring out what breaks.

Eran Gery, an OMG SysML v2 spec contributor, published a practitioner-focused comparison covering the KerML metamodel departure from UML, unified behavioural semantics, model-diagram separation, API-native versioning with commits and branches, and the OSLC and JSON interchange mechanisms for interoperability.

The key takeaway: this is a metamodel replacement, and teams mid-programme on v1 need to treat it that way when scoping a v2 transition.

## Uncertainty Modeling for SysML v2 (PSUM-SysMLv2) — arXiv:2602.21641

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

SysML v2 has better formal semantics than v1, and no native way to represent uncertainty.

For teams modelling cyber-physical or autonomous systems, that gap matters. Uncertainty about sensor reliability, environmental conditions, or system behaviour under degraded modes belongs in the model. Right now, most teams handle it in annotations, separate documents, or not at all.

This arXiv preprint proposes a tested solution: integrating OMG's PSUM uncertainty standard directly into the SysML v2 metamodel as a first-class language extension that follows v2 metamodel conventions. Seven case studies validate the approach across different uncertainty types and system domains.

If you're doing MBSE on autonomous or safety-critical systems and uncertainty currently lives outside your model, this is worth reading.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (The adoption gap)

The SysML v2 spec formalised a lot. Uncertainty representation didn't make the cut, and that gap is now a research problem.

This preprint exposes a structural distance between the v2 spec and what autonomous systems programmes actually need. Uncertainty quantification isn't a niche requirement. For space, autonomous vehicles, and any system operating in partially observable environments, it's a core modelling concern. The fact that a separate standards body effort (OMG PSUM) now fills this gap, nearly two years after SysML v2's adoption, says something about the spec's current scope.

The seven-case-study validation in this paper is encouraging. But until something like PSUM-SysMLv2 enters the normative spec or a widely adopted profile, uncertainty modelling stays a per-programme improvisation exercise.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Uncertainty is not an edge case, and SysML v2 launched without any native way to model it.

Autonomous systems, space missions, and complex CPS programmes all require explicit uncertainty representation at the architecture level. The community celebrated the KerML foundation, the API, and the diagram-model separation, and mostly didn't ask what the spec still couldn't express.

PSUM-SysMLv2 is good research. But the need for it reflects a pattern: the SE community standardises structure and defers semantics. We deferred it with v1's loose behavioural definitions, and the v2 spec repeated the same move. A language extension paper published two years post-adoption shouldn't be the first time uncertainty shows up in a SysML v2 discussion. That conversation was available during the spec development cycle.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown

SysML v2 formalised a lot. Uncertainty representation wasn't part of it.

This February arXiv preprint proposes PSUM-SysMLv2: an extension that integrates OMG's Probability and Statistics for Uncertainty Modelling (PSUM) standard directly into the SysML v2 metamodel. The authors validated the approach across seven case studies covering different uncertainty types and system domains.

The significance for practitioners: this fills a real gap. SysML v2 has no native first-class constructs for expressing uncertainty, which limits its use on autonomous systems, probabilistic safety analysis, and any CPS programme where model inputs carry known variability.

The honest caution: this is a preprint, not a ratified extension, and adoption depends on tool vendors and the OMG standardisation process both moving. Neither moves quickly.

Worth tracking if uncertainty modelling is on your programme's critical path.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641
