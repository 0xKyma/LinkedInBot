## Obeo's AI-Agent-Ready Capella + Capella 7.1 / SysON 2026.5

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Capella is about to become an AI-addressable endpoint. That changes what your governance has to cover.

Obeo announced direct contributions to Capella 7.1 and a plan to make Capella agent-ready through MCP and APIs. The idea is that you connect your own AI ecosystem, Claude, Mistral, Codex, or a local model, in a controlled and auditable way. First commercial release is planned for end of July 2026.

The technical part is straightforward. The hard part is deciding what an agent is allowed to write into your authoritative model, and how you review it before it lands.

If you use Capella today, start drafting that policy now. The tooling will arrive before your process does.

Who owns the review gate when an AI proposes a change to your model?

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
The money is going into making MBSE tools agent-ready, not into making them easier to use.

Obeo's Capella announcement is a good signal. The commitment covers Capella 7.1 contributions and a SaaS SysON offering, but the headline is an MCP and API layer that lets organisations plug their own LLMs into Capella. Commercial release targeted for end of July 2026.

That tells you where vendors think the next round of value sits. Not in the modelling UI. In turning the model into something an external AI can read and modify programmatically.

This is a bet that the model becomes infrastructure other tools query, rather than a place engineers sit and draw. The vendors moving first on the agent interface will set the terms everyone else integrates against.

Watch which tools ship a real agent API in 2026. That list will tell you who is serious.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
An AI-writable authoritative model only works if you already have model governance you can trust. Almost nobody does.

Obeo's plan to make Capella agent-ready through MCP and APIs is genuinely useful. Connect your own LLM, in an auditable way, first commercial release end of July 2026. The architecture is sound.

But the excitement skips over a precondition. If your model already holds authority across the programme, and your review process already catches bad changes, then adding an agent is a real gain. If your model drifts from reality and nobody reviews changes rigorously, the agent just adds a faster way to insert plausible errors.

Most programmes I have seen are closer to the second case than the first. The AI endpoint does not fix that. It exposes it.

Sort out your model governance first. Give an agent write access second.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Capella is getting an AI agent interface, and that raises a question the community has not answered yet.

Obeo's CEO announced direct contributions to Capella 7.1, a web-based SysON SaaS offering, and a plan to make Capella agent-ready via MCP and APIs. Organisations connect their preferred models, Claude, Mistral, Codex, or local ones, in a controlled and auditable way. First commercial release is planned for end of July 2026.

For anyone evaluating AI-augmented MBSE, this is concrete. A named architecture and a near-term date, not a vision slide.

The complication is accountability. When an agent proposes a change to your authoritative model, responsibility splits across the engineer, the AI, and your governance policy. Auditable helps, but auditable is not the same as reviewed.

The capability looks solid. Whether it helps depends entirely on the process you wrap around it.

Would you let an agent write to your model with review, or only read from it?

## INCOSE IS 2026 — "Refocusing MBSE on Practical Systems Engineering"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
A presentation at INCOSE IS 2026 said the quiet part out loud: many MBSE programmes staff for SysML proficiency over engineering depth.

The argument is that a sophisticated model can hide a verification deficiency rather than surface it. Aggressive digital engineering timelines push teams to hire people who can drive the tool, and the systems engineering judgement gets thin.

I have seen this on programmes. The model looks complete. The interfaces are drawn. But nobody on the team can tell you why a requirement exists or what happens when it fails.

A clean diagram is not evidence of a sound design. It is evidence that someone knows the tool.

When you staff your next model-based programme, weight engineering judgement over tool speed. You can teach the tool.

Source: incose.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Presentation-106.pdf

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
Digital engineering timelines are quietly reshaping who gets hired onto systems teams, and not for the better.

The IS 2026 presentation makes a specific claim. Programmes under pressure hire for SysML tool certification because a tool skill is easy to test and fast to onboard. Engineering depth is slower to assess and slower to build, so it loses.

Over a few hiring cycles, that changes the shape of a team. You end up with people who can populate a model but fewer who can challenge whether the design is right.

The presentation's line lands: classically trained systems engineers learn SysML far faster than a modeller becomes an effective systems engineer.

The transition to model-based work created a demand for tool skills. The risk is treating tool skills as a substitute for the engineering they were supposed to serve.

Which are you optimising your hiring for right now?
