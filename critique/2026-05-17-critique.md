## Item A — Agent-based automatic generation of valid SysML v2 models (Computers in Industry, Aug 2025)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Auto-generated SysML v2 that passes a parser tells you almost nothing useful about model quality.

Researchers published an agent-based framework using RAG retrieval and ANTLR grammar enforcement to generate SysML v2 models from natural language prompts. Their headline result: 100% syntactic validity across 20 test prompts.

That is a genuine technical achievement. Getting SysML v2 syntax right consistently is hard, and grammar-constrained generation is a smart architectural choice.

But a parser accepts structure, not meaning. It cannot tell you whether the model captures the right system behaviour, allocates functions correctly, or would survive a peer review. Those are the checks that matter on a programme.

MBSE leads evaluating AI-assisted tooling should be asking vendors one specific question: what is your semantic validation story, and how do you measure it?

### Option 2 — The displacement: what transition risk does AI-generated modelling create?

AI tools that generate syntactically valid SysML v2 are arriving before we have agreed on what a semantically correct model even looks like.

A recent Computers in Industry paper demonstrates RAG plus grammar-enforcement producing 100% syntactic validity for SysML v2 model generation. The engineering is solid. But the paper evaluates outputs against a parser, not against a systems architecture review board.

The displacement risk here is real. Junior modellers learning on AI-assisted tools will internalise that a green parser means a good model. That assumption will take years to unlearn, and programmes will absorb the cost.

We went through something similar when auto-generated SysML v1 diagrams became trivially easy to produce. The result was more diagrams, not better models. The underlying problem did not go away; it just had nicer formatting.

The community needs semantic validation benchmarks before these tools scale into production programmes.

### Option 3 — Contrarian: this solves the wrong problem

Syntax errors are not what slow MBSE programmes down. So why are we celebrating tools that eliminate them?

A new paper in Computers in Industry achieves 100% syntactic validity for AI-generated SysML v2 models using grammar-constrained agents. The authors frame this as a significant step toward automating MBSE. It is technically impressive. But experienced modellers are already fast at producing syntactically correct SysML.

What slows programmes down is ambiguous requirements that no one has resolved, interface definitions that three teams interpret differently, and architectural decisions that exist only in someone's head. None of those problems appear in a parser log.

The bottleneck in MBSE has always been eliciting the right information, agreeing on it, and keeping it current. A grammar agent does not help with any of that.

We are building better hammers for a problem that needs better conversations.

## Item B — RAND "The Impact of Digital Engineering on Defense Acquisition" (RRA2333-2)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

RAND surveyed 300+ aerospace and defence firms on digital engineering, and the headline finding is one you should read carefully before your next programme review.

DE has not yet produced notable cost savings or schedule reductions. A DoD-sponsored study drawing on industry responses across hundreds of firms reached that conclusion.

This does not mean DE is without value. The report does find other benefits: improved information sharing, better cross-team visibility, earlier identification of integration issues. Those matter. But they are not the metrics most programme offices use to justify DE investment.

If your programme is selling DE on cost and schedule, this report is a problem for that argument. And if your organisation cannot define what metrics leadership uses to track DE success, you are not alone: more than half of surveyed firms said the same.

Read the full report before someone uses it against you in a budget review.

Source: https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2333-2.html

### Option 2 — The money signal: what does this reveal about where DE investment is actually going?

A RAND report funded by the Air Force says digital engineering has not yet produced measurable cost savings or schedule reductions. The programmes keep running. The investment keeps flowing. That gap is worth examining.

The study covers 300+ firms. It finds DE delivering value in collaboration and visibility, but not in the acquisition metrics that drive budget decisions. Simultaneously, more than half of surveyed organisations cannot articulate what their own leadership uses to measure DE success.

When investment continues to scale despite a null result on its primary stated objectives, one of three things is usually happening: the benefits are real but lagging, the metrics are wrong, or compliance mandates are driving spend rather than outcomes.

All three may be true simultaneously here. DoD mandates are pulling DE adoption faster than organisations can build the competency to extract value from it. That is a structural problem, and no amount of tooling solves it.

### Option 3 — Contrarian: the community is avoiding the real conversation here

A DoD-sponsored RAND study finds that digital engineering has not yet produced measurable cost savings or schedule reductions. The SE community's response has largely been: "we just need more time and better implementation."

That framing is too comfortable.

The RAND finding covers years of investment across hundreds of firms. At some point, "we need more time" stops being a reasonable response and becomes a way of deferring accountability. If DE were producing clear value on the metrics programmes are measured against, we would see it by now in at least some of the data.

A harder reading: DE as currently practised optimises for model richness and information visibility, which are genuine goods, but does not directly address the coordination failures, requirement volatility, and supply chain fragility that actually drive cost growth and schedule slip.

Better models of a broken process are still a broken process.

## Item C — SysTemp: Multi-Agent System for Template-Based Generation of SysML v2 (arXiv / Siemens, June 2025)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Four specialist LLM agents, a template library, and a parser validation loop. That is the architecture Siemens researchers built to get syntactically valid SysML v2 out of natural language input, and it still required all of that scaffolding to work reliably.

The SysTemp paper is worth reading if you are evaluating AI-assisted modelling tools, because it is honest about why single-prompt LLM approaches fail on SysML v2: there is not enough v2 syntax in public training corpora. The language is too new. The models hallucinate plausible-looking but invalid constructs.

The template-based architecture works around that by constraining generation to known-valid patterns. That is a pragmatic fix. But it also means the system cannot model anything that falls outside its template library, which limits it for novel or complex system architectures.

Know what you are buying when a vendor tells you their tool uses AI to generate SysML v2.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21608

### World Event Option 1 — Verification Gap / Bridging Capability

Cancelling the E-7 Wedgetail made sense on paper. It makes less sense when the replacement architecture isn't verified yet.

The original decision rested on an assumption: space-based sensors would absorb the Airborne Moving Target Indicator mission before the E-3 fleet ran out of life. That assumption was never operationally validated. The Iran conflict ran the experiment instead, and the answer came back negative.

What makes this pattern worth examining is the compounding logic. E-7 delays were cited as justification for cancellation. But the programme replacing it was treated as if delays weren't a risk. One system's schedule uncertainty counted against it. The other's didn't count at all.

In any rigorous capability architecture review, retiring a verified incumbent depends on the successor being verified for the same operational need, not just planned to be. That gap between "planned" and "verified" is where bridging capability disappears.

The reversal is the right call. The fact that it took a conflict to force it is the part worth sitting with.

### World Event Option 2 — Unintended Consequences / Emergence

A programme gets cancelled because it's delayed and a future system will replace it. Then the future system isn't ready. Then the original programme gets revived. This is not a procurement failure. It's an emergent consequence of how assumptions propagate through a capability architecture.

The E-7 Wedgetail reversal is a clean example. The "divest to invest" logic treated the two systems as sequential: retire the old one, the new one arrives, the mission continues. But real systems don't hand off cleanly. They overlap, slip, and interact in ways the planning model doesn't capture.

The feedback loop here ran in the wrong direction. E-7 delays reduced confidence in the programme, which drove cancellation, which created a capability gap, which the Iran conflict exposed, which forced reinstatement. Each step was locally rational. The outcome wasn't.

When you design out a bridging capability, you're implicitly betting that the transition will go to plan. Operational environments don't honour that bet.
