## SysML v2 ISO Submission + SysML 2.1 / KerML 1.1 RTF Establishment

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

SysML v2.0 is now formally stable. The ISO submission is done. So should you start planning around it?

The March 2026 release packages KerML 1.0, SysML 2.0, and the Systems Modelling API 1.0 as the submitted ISO versions. Minor editorial changes, SVG image replacements. The spec content itself is locked.

At the same time, the OMG has established Revision Task Forces for KerML 1.1, SysML 2.1, and API 1.1. No resolutions have been approved yet, but the correction cycle has started.

For practitioners, this is a usable signal. The v2.0 baseline is solid enough to plan tooling adoption against. You are not waiting for a moving target. But you should also expect incremental corrections to arrive, so build your adoption timeline with that in mind.

The question worth asking now: does your organisation have a plan for what happens when v2.1 lands mid-adoption?

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the displacement)

SysML v2 is replacing v1. The ISO submission makes that transition official. The transition risk is real and mostly undiscussed.

The March 2026 release formalises v2.0 as the submitted ISO standard. Simultaneously, RTFs for v2.1 and KerML 1.1 are open, which means the correction cycle starts before most organisations have finished evaluating v2.0 tooling.

This creates a specific displacement problem. Organisations still running SysML v1.x models now sit formally two versions behind a published standard. Tools that only support v1 occupy a legacy position, and that gap will widen as procurement language catches up to the standard.

The money signal here is also clear. The OMG invested in RTFs immediately after ISO submission. That pace suggests the standards body expects uptake pressure from procurement and contracting within 12 to 18 months.

If your programme is still on v1 tooling, the window for a managed transition is narrowing.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Submitting SysML v2 to ISO gets treated as an adoption trigger. I think it changes less than people expect.

The ISO submission locks editorial front matter and replaces PNG images with SVG. The technical content of KerML 1.0 and SysML 2.0 is unchanged. The spec sat publicly available and implementable for months before submission. Tooling support is still patchy. Training material is still thin. Organisations that needed ISO status to justify adoption will find another reason to wait.

Organisations already building on v2 did not need ISO submission to start. Organisations holding back have concerns that spec maturity does not address: tooling gaps, workforce readiness, and migration cost.

RTFs for v2.1 are already open with zero approved resolutions. That is worth watching carefully. Adopting v2.0 tooling now means absorbing whatever corrections v2.1 introduces before the ink is dry on your modelling standards.

ISO status carries weight in procurement language. For actual adoption readiness, the blockers are workforce, tooling, and migration planning.

## "Workflow-Level Design Principles for Trustworthy GenAI in Automotive SE" — arXiv 2602.19614

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Feeding a large requirements specification to an LLM in one prompt and asking it to update your SysML v2 model misses changes. A recent paper quantifies this and offers a fix.

The arXiv paper (2602.19614) builds an end-to-end automotive pipeline: identify requirement deltas, propagate changes into a SysML v2 architecture, re-run tests. The finding that matters is that monolithic prompting degrades completeness and correctness on large specs. Section-wise decomposition improves both, and a compilation and static analysis step catches errors in the outputs.

This is directly applicable to anyone experimenting with AI-assisted modelling. The specific failure mode, big-bang prompting on large documents, is also the most natural way to try this for the first time.

The paper makes a constrained claim: with the right workflow structure, the outputs are traceable and verifiable enough to be useful. That is a more honest framing than most GenAI plus SE content produces.

Read it before you design your own AI-assisted modelling workflow.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19614

### Track 2: World Events

### World Event Option 1 — System Lifecycle Planning Frame

The T-38 Talon was designed for 7,000 flight hours. Many of the grounded aircraft have passed 20,000. That is not a sustainment success. That is a design envelope breach that accumulated over decades.

The fleet-wide stand-down after the May 12 Mississippi crash is being reported as a safety event. From a systems engineering standpoint, it is the visible consequence of a lifecycle planning failure that was years in the making.

The T-7A Red Hawk reached Milestone C on April 23, enabling the first 14 production aircraft. It will not reach Air Education and Training Command until 2027 at the earliest. The T-38 replacement was already years late before this grounding.

Two programme timelines failed to interlock. The retirement of one system and the fielding of its replacement were managed as a scheduling problem rather than a transition engineering problem. The gap between them is now operationally real.

If your programme has a legacy system operating beyond its designed envelope, the question to ask is whether the transition plan is actually resourced and sequenced, or just drawn on a slide.

### World Event Option 2 — Systems Thinking / Emergence Frame

A system operating outside its designed envelope will eventually produce an outcome the designers never modelled. The T-38 fleet grounding is that outcome.

The T-38 was retired in design terms long before it was retired in practice. Each life-extension decision was locally rational: the replacement was delayed, the mission need remained, and extending the existing platform was cheaper than the alternative. Repeated often enough, those local decisions produced a fleet where 55.3% readiness in 2024 was considered acceptable, and where a crash grounds every advanced jet trainer the Air Force operates.

No single decision caused this. The grounding is an emergent property of accumulated deferral across budget cycles, programme offices, and administrations. Each node in the system made a defensible call. The system as a whole moved steadily toward a state nobody chose.

This pattern shows up in infrastructure, in software, and in any programme where end-of-life planning competes with near-term cost pressure. The exits close slowly, and then all at once.

How does your organisation treat the interface between a retiring system and its replacement? Schedule dependency or engineered transition?
