## SysTemp — Multi-Agent LLM for SysML v2 Generation (C1)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

GPT-4 and Llama 3.1-70B both fail at generating valid SysML v2 from natural language. That finding came out of a published arXiv paper, and it matches what practitioners have been noticing in practice.

The SysTemp paper proposes a multi-agent workaround: a TemplateGeneratorAgent structures the output, and a ParserAgent iteratively corrects syntax errors. The result is syntactically valid SysML v2 textual notation where raw prompting produces garbage.

Raw LLMs fail here for a straightforward reason. The SysML v2 training corpus barely exists. The spec is new, the textual syntax is unfamiliar to every major model, and there is no large body of correct examples to learn from.

SysTemp is a scaffold over a gap that won't close quickly. If you're evaluating AI-assisted modelling tools, this paper tells you exactly what question to ask the vendor: how are you handling the corpus problem?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21608

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the displacement)

Every MBSE tooling vendor is currently adding AI-assisted modelling features. The SysTemp paper explains why most of them are probably overpromising.

Raw LLM performance on SysML v2 textual notation is poor, even with frontier models. GPT-4 and Llama 3.1-70B both produced syntactically invalid output in baseline tests. The multi-agent architecture SysTemp proposes gets around this with structured templates and iterative error correction, but that's a significant engineering effort on top of the base model.

The money signal: any vendor claiming their LLM integration generates reliable SysML v2 models today is either using a heavily scaffolded approach they haven't fully disclosed, or they're working with SysML v1 constructs and calling it v2-compatible.

That distinction matters if you're selecting tooling for a programme that needs to run on the v2 spec.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21608

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Multi-agent scaffolding that forces an LLM to generate valid SysML v2 is technically interesting. Whether it solves the right problem is a separate question.

SysTemp gets the syntax right. The TemplateGeneratorAgent structures the output, the ParserAgent corrects errors, and the result validates against the spec. That's a genuine engineering contribution.

But syntactic validity is the easiest part of MBSE. The hard part is semantic correctness: does the model actually represent the system? Does the interface definition match the real interface? Does the requirement the LLM extracted from the natural language spec say what the engineer intended?

A model that parses cleanly and captures the wrong behaviour is worse than a document that admits its own ambiguity. We've spent years telling people that MBSE adds rigour. An AI-generated model that looks rigorous but hasn't been verified by someone who understands the system doesn't add rigour. It hides the absence of it.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21608

## IEEE — Graph-Theoretic Partitioning and MBSE Model Metrics (C3)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Three quantifiable metrics for MBSE model quality: system complexity, modularity, and test effort. Derived from functional flow block diagrams using graph theory and community detection. Applied to real automotive propulsion systems.

That's what a 2026 IEEE paper is offering, and for most MBSE leads I know, it addresses a genuine pain point. Programme managers ask whether the model is good. We usually answer with a description of what it covers. A structural metric gives us something more defensible.

The methodology works by transforming system functions into a functional dependence graph, then applying community detection to identify partitions. The three metrics fall out of that structure.

It won't work for every model type. If your architecture is primarily requirements-driven rather than function-flow-driven, the FFBD basis may not fit. But for integration-heavy programmes where functional decomposition is the core modelling activity, this is worth reading.

Source: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11224723/

#MBSE #SystemsEngineering #SysML

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the adjacent threat)

Software engineering has had code quality metrics for decades. Cyclomatic complexity, coupling, cohesion. MBSE has had almost none. This IEEE paper starts to close that gap, and it matters more than it looks.

The methodology derives complexity, modularity, and test effort estimates directly from functional model structure using community detection on a dependence graph. The three case studies include automotive propulsion systems, which gives it industrial credibility beyond a toy example.

The adjacent threat is that software quality tooling communities have been moving into model-quality territory for several years. If MBSE practitioners don't develop credible, repeatable metrics for model quality, that problem will get solved by people optimising for software architecture, and the resulting metrics won't reflect systems engineering concerns.

This paper is one contribution toward a practitioner-owned answer. The SE community should be building on it, not waiting for the software side to colonise the space.

### Track 2: World Events

### World Event Option 1 — Verification gap / autonomous system state modelling

The YFQ-42A didn't crash because of a code bug. It crashed because the software's model of the aircraft was wrong at the moment of flight.

The autopilot miscalculated weight and centre of gravity. GA-ASI and the Air Force traced it, implemented a software fix, and returned to flight on May 22. The official framing: "failing forward." This is exactly why we test.

That framing deserves scrutiny.

CoG as a function of fuel state, store configuration, and loading is not unknowable before flight. For a conventional aircraft, we verify it analytically. For an autonomous platform, the autopilot's internal state model *is* a live operational parameter, and it changes with every configuration. That makes it a requirements problem, not just a test problem.

If the software's assumed system state is wrong, V&V against a nominal model will not catch it. We need to verify the state estimation logic itself, across the full configuration envelope, before we put the aircraft in the air.

"Test to discover" is acceptable for emergent behaviour. For physics, it should not be our first line of defence.

Source: https://breakingdefense.com/2026/05/general-atomics-cca-drone-returns-to-flight/

#SystemsEngineering #MBSE #AutonomousSystems

---

### World Event Option 2 — Unintended consequences / model-reality feedback

An autonomous aircraft crashed because its software believed the aircraft weighed something different from what it actually weighed.

That sentence sounds simple. The implications are not.

For a piloted aircraft, the human in the loop continuously reconciles model and reality. They feel the aircraft behave unexpectedly and they adapt. Remove the pilot and that feedback loop is gone. The software's internal model of the physical system becomes the only ground truth the controller acts on. If that model drifts from reality, there is no corrective signal until the physics reasserts itself.

This is an emergence problem. The failure did not exist in the software in isolation, or in the airframe in isolation. It existed in the gap between the two, at runtime, under a specific configuration the model did not reflect.

The Air Force is calling it "failing forward." Col. Helfrich is right that the programme was structured to learn. But the deeper question is whether we are building the feedback mechanisms into autonomous system architectures that let the software *know* when its model of the physical world has become unreliable.

Right now, for most platforms, we are not.

Source: https://www.airandspaceforces.com/yfq-42a-cca-returns-to-flight-software-fix-crash/
