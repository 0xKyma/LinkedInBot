## OMG SysMLv2 Certification Program Launch

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

OMG just launched the first official SysMLv2 certification, and the sequencing tells you something.

The exam available now is "Model User" — reading and interpreting SysML v2 models. The "Model Builder Fundamental" tier, for people who actually author models, arrives in early 2027 at the earliest. The credentialling ladder starts with consumers, not producers.

Given where tooling sits today, that order makes some sense. But it means a practising MBSE engineer who pursues this now earns a credential for a capability they likely already have, in a language their tools may not yet fully support.

My take: the Model User cert makes most sense for programme managers and reviewers who need to engage with v2 outputs, and for engineers building the internal case for v2 transition. For hands-on modellers, I'd wait for the Builder tier unless your organisation already runs v2 natively.

Is your team treating this as a hiring signal, a training milestone, or something to revisit in 2027?

The U.S. Department of War is the Diamond Sponsor of the new OMG SysMLv2 certification programme. Dassault Systèmes, Lockheed Martin, and Sierra Nevada Corporation sit at Platinum and Gold tiers.

Read that list twice.

When a certification launches with defence prime and major vendor backing before the authoring-tier exam even exists, that reflects institutional pre-positioning, not organic community demand. The sponsors are signalling that SysML v2 competency is moving toward a programme requirement, and they want the credentialling infrastructure in place before the workforce scrambles to catch up.

For engineers at defence contractors, v2 literacy is shifting from "good to have" toward something your BD and staffing teams will start listing on proposals. The timing of the certification may be early. The direction of travel is not ambiguous.

The Builder tier lands in early 2027. Between now and then, watch the distance between certified Model Users and programmes that can actually build in v2.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Certifying people to read SysML v2 models before most tools can build them reliably is a strange place to start.

The OMG SysMLv2 Model User certification launched this week. It tests your ability to interpret v2 models. The Model Builder exam, covering actual authoring, arrives in early 2027 at best.

The community is excited. I'm more cautious.

Certification programmes create demand for training content, which creates incentives to teach the spec rather than applied practice. We saw this with v1. Engineers held OCSMP credentials and couldn't construct a coherent IBD from a real requirements set. The credential and the capability drifted apart because the exams tested language knowledge, not modelling judgement.

SysML v2 is a better language. But launching a Model User exam ahead of a Model Builder exam, before production tooling stabilises, risks producing a wave of people who can discuss v2 fluently and model in it poorly. The incentive structure worries me more than the exam content.

Source: https://www.omg.org/certification/sysml2/

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown

OMG launched its official SysMLv2 Certification Programme on June 2, and it's the first credential series tied specifically to the new standard.

The programme opens with the "SysMLv2 Model User" exam, covering how to read and interpret v2 models. A "Model Builder Fundamental" tier for authoring follows in early 2027. The DoW holds Diamond Sponsorship, with Dassault Systèmes, Lockheed Martin, and Sierra Nevada at Platinum and Gold — institutional backing that signals this is moving toward a programme requirement in defence acquisition, not just a professional development option.

For practitioners, the credential has immediate relevance for engineers and managers who need to engage with v2 model outputs before their organisations fully build in v2.

One honest complication: tooling support for SysML v2 is still maturing. Past credential cycles in this community produced engineers who knew the spec and struggled with the practice. Whether this one avoids that outcome depends on how the Builder tier is scoped and what applied competency it actually requires.

## NDIA/INCOSE/IEEE 2026 MBSE Symposium

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

The 2026 MBSE Symposium technical programme signals a real shift in what this community considers worth publishing.

Papers on AI-accelerated risk assessment using MBSE models, agentic software synthesis from system models, and UAV mission MBSE sit alongside a paper titled "Systems Engineering 2.0: Toward an Empirical Foundation Through Digital Engineering." Three or four years ago, most symposium papers were still making the case for model-based over document-based approaches. That debate has largely left the agenda.

The empirical foundations paper is the one I'd prioritise. The community has asserted MBSE value for years. Putting "empirical foundation" in a paper title suggests at least some people want data instead of arguments.

If you're in Huntsville, I'd genuinely like to know how that paper lands in the room. The proceedings will be worth tracking when they publish.

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (The adoption gap)

"Transforming the Digital Enterprise" is the 2026 MBSE Symposium theme. The specific technical content underneath it is more revealing than the theme itself.

Papers on empirical foundations for SE, agentic software synthesis from models, and AI-assisted MBSE risk assessment point to a community starting to feel the distance between MBSE as a discipline and MBSE as a measurable contributor to programme outcomes.

Programmes have had capable tools and trained practitioners for years. The persistent gap sits in governance: models that exist but don't drive decisions, digital threads that connect artefacts but not accountabilities, organisations that adopted the vocabulary without changing the workflow. The fact that "empirical foundation" is a keynote-adjacent topic this year suggests the community is starting to name that gap more directly.

Whether the papers deliver evidence or just better-constructed arguments for familiar positions is what makes the proceedings worth reading carefully.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

"Transforming the Digital Enterprise" has been a symposium-worthy theme for at least five consecutive years. At some point, transformation that's always upcoming needs to be examined.

The 2026 NDIA/INCOSE/IEEE MBSE Symposium opens today in Huntsville with keynotes from Sanford Friedenthal and Ed Seidewitz, two of the people who have shaped this community's direction most significantly. The agenda is strong. The papers on agentic synthesis and empirical SE foundations are genuinely interesting.

But the symposium circuit has a structural problem: it consistently surfaces what the research and vendor community wants to talk about, not what practitioners on running programmes are actually stuck on. Papers on LLM-assisted MBSE and digital thread expansion coexist with programmes that still can't get a consistent interface definition into a model and keep it there.

The community is not short of ideas. It's short of honest after-action content from programmes that tried MBSE at scale and ran into organisational or contractual walls. That work rarely makes it to a symposium paper.

What would it look like if the 2027 theme was "What Didn't Work and Why"?
