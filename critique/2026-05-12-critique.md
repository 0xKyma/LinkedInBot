## Item 1: "AI as Consumer and Participant: A Co-Design Agenda for MBSE Substrates and Methodology" (arXiv:2604.25526)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Your SysML model is not helping your AI tool reason. It is helping it hallucinate more confidently.

A preprint submitted last week makes a point that most AI-plus-MBSE conversations miss. When a frontier LLM queries a conformant SysML model, the reasoning it produces draws from training data, not from the model itself. Nobody designed that model to function as a machine-queryable knowledge substrate. It serves human navigation, and that is all it does.

So the AI produces output that looks grounded. And you have no reliable way to tell whether it retrieved anything real from your model or just pattern-matched its way to a plausible answer.

The authors argue the fix requires co-designing the model and the methodology together, from the start, with AI consumption as an explicit design requirement. Retrofitting is not enough.

If you are currently integrating AI tooling over an existing SysML model, that is worth sitting with.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 2 — Industry angle: the displacement risk

We are about to repeat the requirements tool mistake, but with AI.

Organisations spent years stuffing requirements into tools built for document management. Those tools shaped the practice in ways nobody intended, and we are still untangling it.

A new arXiv preprint argues we are on the same trajectory with AI and MBSE. Teams layer AI tools over models that human navigators built for human navigation. Nobody restructured the underlying substrate to support machine querying. The tooling changes. The substrate does not. And the methodology governing how engineers build models has not changed either.

The authors call for co-designing the model, the methodology, and the AI participation requirements together. That is a significant ask. It means the modelling decisions you make today are also AI architecture decisions, whether you treat them that way or not.

The organisations that figure this out first will have a structural advantage that is very hard to close later.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle: the community is avoiding the real conversation

Plugging AI into your existing MBSE practice produces an experiment with real liability, whether you have named that liability or not.

The SE community has largely framed AI adoption as a tooling question. Better co-pilots. Smarter query interfaces. Assistants that read your model and answer questions about it.

A preprint from last week challenges that framing directly. The authors show that LLMs querying SysML models produce reasoning drawn from training data. Two different tools over the same conformant model produce different results. The model is not the knowledge source. It is decoration on top of a black box.

The community keeps building better decoration.

The actual work, designing models and methodologies to function as machine-queryable substrates, has barely started. And engineers cannot bolt it on after the fact.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

## Item 2: "Why MBSE Still Breaks at the Seams and How SysML v2 Could Help" (MathWorks, 30 April 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Most SysML v2 migration arguments focus on syntax. The stronger argument is about plumbing.

The MathWorks digital engineering blog published a piece last week worth taking seriously. MBSE keeps fragmenting at programme level because every tool integration is a bespoke, brittle, point-to-point connection. Requirements live in one place. Architecture in another. Analysis somewhere else. Verification evidence sits in yet another system, maintained by a different team.

SysML v2 introduces a RESTful API and a repository-centred exchange model. Instead of twenty direct integrations between ten tools, you get tools connecting through a common mechanism.

The post is honest that this does not guarantee a digital thread on its own. The API is an enabler, not an outcome. But it changes the plumbing in a way that actually makes the problem tractable.

If your current argument for SysML v2 migration centres on syntax improvements, you may be underselling it to the people who hold the budget.

Source: https://blogs.mathworks.com/digitaleng/2026/04/30/why-mbse-still-breaks-at-the-seams-and-how-sysml-v2-could-help/

That is an infrastructure argument, not a modelling argument. It targets programme managers and enterprise architects, not just modellers.

If SysML v2 adoption accelerates on the strength of that infrastructure promise, the tools that own the repository layer will own the programme.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle: this solves the wrong problem

SysML v2's API gives you cleaner infrastructure for a fragmentation problem that has never been technical.

The MathWorks post this week is well-argued and I agree with the diagnosis: most MBSE implementations are a patchwork of disconnected tools with no coherent data exchange. The SysML v2 RESTful API is a cleaner mechanism than what we have now.

But programmes fragment because different teams own different tools, carry different incentives, and nobody holds authority to enforce a shared data model across all of them. A better API gives you infrastructure for integration that nobody is mandated to use.

We have had integration standards before. Most programmes ignored them because the teams who needed to align never aligned, and no governance structure forced them to.

SysML v2 is worth adopting. But if your current tool fragmentation comes from unclear ownership and misaligned incentives, a new API spec will not move you.

### Track 2: World Events

### World Event Option 1 — Verification Gap / Requirements Traceability Frame

The global oil reserve system passed every test. That's exactly why it failed operationally.

When Iran restricted Strait of Hormuz traffic earlier this year, IEA member countries released 400 million barrels of emergency reserves in days. Fast, coordinated, on spec. The system did what the requirements said.

But world consumption runs at roughly 105 million barrels per day. That release covered about four days of global demand, and around 20 days of normal Strait flows. The reserve architecture targeted short-duration supply shocks, not a sustained closure of a single critical node.

The bypass pipeline routes exist on paper. Nobody load-tested them at operational scale, under real supply chain conditions. That's an untested interface sitting inside a verified system.

In programme work we call this a verification gap: your system satisfies every requirement, but the requirements described a scenario that didn't survive contact with reality.

Passing V&V and being fit for the actual use case are not the same thing.

Source: https://www.iea.org/topics/the-middle-east-and-global-energy-markets

### World Event Option 2 — Systems Thinking / Emergence and Complexity Frame

A system can behave exactly as designed and still produce catastrophic outcomes. The global energy architecture just demonstrated this.

The Strait of Hormuz handles around 20% of global oil flows through a channel roughly 50 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. Every analyst knew this was a critical node. Redundant routes exist. Emergency reserves exist. The response mechanisms worked.

What nobody adequately modelled was the interaction between closure duration, reserve depletion rate, and the logistics capacity needed to reroute meaningful volumes through bypass infrastructure that has never operated at scale under pressure.

The resilience looked real in normal conditions. Under stress, the redundant path turned out to be theoretical. That's emergence: a system-level vulnerability that no single component reveals, and that only the interaction between components under a stressed environment brings to the surface.

Designing for robustness under anticipated conditions is a much lower bar than achieving robustness under the conditions that actually arrive.

Source: https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/3/15/strategic-oil-release-may-calm-markets-but-cannot-fix-hormuz-disruption

#SystemsThinking #SystemsEngineering #Resilience
