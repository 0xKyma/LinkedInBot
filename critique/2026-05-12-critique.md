### Track 1: MBSE / SysML / Systems Engineering

## Item 1 — "AI as Consumer and Participant: A Co-Design Agenda for MBSE Substrates and Methodology" (arXiv:2604.25526)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Most teams feed their SysML models into AI tools without asking what the AI actually does with them.

A recent arXiv preprint by Siyuan Ji makes a specific argument: when LLMs reason over a SysML model today, they draw from training data, not from the model itself. The model functions as a prompt. Different tools over the same model produce different results, and the record contains nothing to adjudicate between them.

Ji's proposed fix goes beyond better tooling. He argues the model substrate and the methodology governing its construction need co-design for AI participation from the start.

I think this is right, and most programmes are nowhere near ready to act on it. The tooling conversation is running years ahead of the modelling methodology conversation.

If your team already uses AI assistance over your SysML models, what are you actually trusting it to retrieve versus generate?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the adjacent threat)

Software engineering is about to solve the "AI over structured models" problem, and their solution will not suit systems engineers.

A new preprint argues that MBSE models currently function as prompts for LLMs rather than as knowledge bases. The reasoning AI produces over these models comes from training, not from the model. Ji calls this a substrate problem: models need to be built for AI consumption from the start.

Software already runs graph databases, semantic layers, and retrieval-augmented pipelines in production. Those tools will reach system models because they exist and work well enough. The result will be AI-assisted SE that reflects software architecture assumptions, not MBSE methodology.

If the systems engineering community does not define what an AI-ready model structure looks like, someone else will define it for us. That definition will ship in a product before any standard catches up.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle (the mainstream fix makes it worse)

Integrating better AI tooling into your MBSE workflow before fixing your model structure makes the problem harder to fix later.

A preprint published this month argues that attaching improved AI tools to existing SysML models does not resolve the core issue: models need to be built for AI consumption before AI tooling can reason faithfully over them. Sophisticated tooling over a poorly structured model produces more fluent, harder-to-catch errors.

The community's current instinct is to improve the tool layer first because tools ship faster than methodology changes. But every programme that embeds AI assistance over an unfit model substrate accumulates technical debt in the methodology itself.

Methodology debt is harder to unwind than tooling debt. Changing tools is a procurement decision. Changing how a programme constructs and governs its models touches every team, every review, and every interface agreement already in place.

The appetite for that conversation does not match the pace at which teams adopt AI tooling.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

## Item 2 — "Enhancing MBSE via Graph-Theoretic Partitioning and System Model Metrics" (IEEE 11224723)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

We talk about model quality constantly. We measure it almost never.

A 2026 IEEE paper proposes three structural metrics derived directly from functional flow block diagrams: system complexity, system modularity, and system test effort. The authors compute these via graph-theoretic community detection and confirm the approach on two industrial automotive propulsion system case studies alongside an academic autonomous mobile robot.

The test effort metric is the one I find most interesting. Deriving predicted test effort from model structure, before any test planning begins, gives programmes something concrete to argue about early.

The obvious pushback is whether these three metrics actually predict programme outcomes. The authors confirm on case studies, not longitudinal programme data, so that question remains open.

But the direction is right. Quantified structural metrics from the model itself, not from subjective review, give a more defensible basis for architecture decisions than the current norm.

Source: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11224723/

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the adoption gap)

MBSE adoption is growing. The ability to evaluate whether an MBSE model is actually good is not keeping pace.

A new IEEE paper derives three structural metrics from system functional models using graph-theoretic partitioning: complexity, modularity, and test effort. The authors confirm the approach on industrial automotive case studies and give programmes a quantitative basis for comparing architectural options within the model, rather than relying on review consensus.

The gap this exposes is real. Most programmes using MBSE today have no formal metric for model quality beyond "does it pass review." That is a governance gap. Reviewers apply experience-based judgement, which is inconsistent across programmes and almost impossible to audit.

Quantified structural metrics would change what programme-level model governance looks like. They would also make it possible to compare models across programmes in a meaningful way, something defence acquirers have wanted for years and have not had a method for.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle (solves the wrong problem)

Graph-theoretic model metrics are technically rigorous and will not fix the reason MBSE models fail programmes.

A recent IEEE paper derives complexity, modularity, and test-effort metrics from functional flow block diagrams. The methodology is sound. Grounding MBSE model evaluation in something measurable is a genuine step forward.

But the structural problems that cause MBSE programmes to underdeliver are not complexity or low modularity scores. They are disagreement about scope, interfaces captured in documents the model does not reference, and requirements that exist in the model and nowhere else in the programme's contractual baseline.

A model can score well on every graph metric and still sit disconnected from how the programme actually runs. Measuring the model's internal structure tells you about the model. It does not tell you whether the model is authoritative, maintained, or trusted by the people making decisions.

I would like to see these metrics applied. I would not replace stakeholder trust audits with them.

Source: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11224723/

## Item 3 — "Advancing MBSE in Naval Vessel Systems Architecture" (Capella vs. CDP4-COMET, INCOSE SE Journal)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Capella outperforms CDP4-COMET in early-stage naval architecture development, and the peer-reviewed evidence for that claim is now published.

A paper in the INCOSE Systems Engineering Journal compares both tools across all four architecture perspectives: operational, functional, logical, and physical. The finding is direct: Capella performs better in the early design stage.

The authors are explicit about what they mean by better. Capella's viewpoint-based architecture coverage and its support for methodical progression through MBSE layers give it an advantage when the design space is still open and the team makes structural decisions, not just documents them.

Nobody disputes CDP4-COMET's concurrent design capabilities. The paper's argument is that those capabilities matter more once the architecture is more settled.

If you are choosing tooling for an early-stage defence programme, this paper gives you a peer-reviewed basis for that conversation.

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70020

### Option 2 — Industry angle (the displacement)

The concurrent design community has invested heavily in CDP4-COMET. A new peer-reviewed paper suggests that investment targets the wrong phase.

The INCOSE Systems Engineering Journal paper on naval vessel architecture compares Capella and CDP4-COMET across early-stage design and concludes Capella performs better before the architecture stabilises. Concurrent design tooling suits a phase that comes after the hard architectural work finishes.

This creates a transition risk that programmes rarely plan for. Teams start in Capella for early architecture, migrate to a concurrent design environment for detailed design, and then find that model continuity between those phases is manual, partial, and expensive to maintain.

The naval domain amplifies this because platform complexity is high and the early-stage decisions about operational and functional architecture constrain everything downstream. Getting the early-stage tooling right matters more there than in domains with shorter design cycles.

The paper is openly accessible via TU Delft under CC BY.

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70020

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle (only works under conditions nobody has)

"Capella performs better in early design" is a defensible finding. The conditions under which it holds are narrower than the paper's framing suggests.

The INCOSE journal comparison gives Capella the early-stage verdict based on viewpoint coverage and methodical architecture progression. That is a fair assessment of the tool's design intent. Capella guides teams through structured MBSE layers well when the team follows the method.

The limiting condition is the team. Capella's early-stage advantage depends on practitioners who understand the Arcadia method and apply it consistently. In most naval defence programmes, that is not the team available at project start. Early design typically draws on domain experts and architects who are not MBSE specialists, working under schedule pressure, with modelling support that arrives later than it should.

Under those conditions, the tool comparison becomes secondary. Methodology literacy is the binding constraint, not tool capability.

Read the paper's verdict as "Capella performs better when teams apply it as intended in early design." That is a much more conditional claim than the headline finding implies.

## Track 2: World Events — Defence / Energy / Geopolitics (SE Lens)

### World Event Option 1 — Requirements Volatility as an Architectural Problem

The US Army's REWSI solicitation contains something unusual: a formal architecture diagnosis of its own EW acquisition system.

The Characteristics of Need states directly that EMSO capabilities are fragmented, non-modular, and not cohesive. That framing matters. When an acquisition document names structural incoherence as the starting condition, the programme has moved past requirements failure into architecture failure.

The underlying SE problem is recognisable: when the threat environment changes faster than the requirements-to-field cycle, the verification artefact goes stale before the system passes it. You can execute the SE process correctly and still field the wrong system.

The Army's response replaces requirement decomposition with capability-centric discovery. Programme managers assess technologies for what they demonstrate today, not through a requirements lens two years old. A commercial library becomes the architecture surrogate.

Whether a curated commercial library constitutes a coherent architecture is a legitimate open question. But the diagnosis in that document is hard to argue with.

Can you do rigorous SE without stable requirements, or does this approach just defer the architecture problem?

### World Event Option 2 — Emergence and Feedback Loops in EW Competition

EW capability cycles in Ukraine run in days. The US Army's acquisition cycle runs in years. That gap reflects a system operating exactly as its designers intended, and that is the problem.

The REWSI solicitation, released 28 April, openly acknowledges that the traditional defence acquisition feedback loop cannot keep pace with the electromagnetic environment. The threat adapts. A counter-adaptation follows. Then the counter-counter-adaptation. By the time a bespoke system clears verification, the loop has already cycled past the threat it targeted.

What the Army proposes is a structural change to the feedback loop itself. A commercial library model shortens the cycle from requirement to fielded capability. It trades optimisation for speed of adaptation.

The risk is real: faster feedback loops in complex systems generate emergent behaviour that architecture reviews do not catch. A loosely coupled commercial capability library, integrated under operational pressure, produces different systemic outcomes than a coherent architecture that teams consciously designed. Those two things will eventually diverge in ways that matter.

The Army knows the current model cannot hold. Whether the replacement model holds under real EW operational complexity remains open.

Source: https://cpeisw.army.mil/2026/05/01/army-to-prototype-new-electromagnetic-warfare-rapid-integration-system-eris/
