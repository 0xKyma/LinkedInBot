## SHIA: SysML Stays in the Verification Loop (arXiv, May 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Most programmes abandon the system model the moment verification hits hardware.

The model governs the implementation right up until HIL testing. Then domain-specific simulation tools, transformation chains, and bespoke integrations take over. The model drifts out of sync with the thing it was supposed to control.

A new arXiv paper introduces SHIA, an architecture that keeps an executable SysML model directly inside the verification loop. It exchanges messages with physical hardware without intermediate transformations or broker-mediated plugins.

If this holds up, model authority starts meaning something at the test bench. Right now that authority quietly stops applying there. Most of us just accept it.

Start by finding where your own model drops out of the V&V chain. That handoff point is where drift begins.

Go map that handoff on your current programme before you cite the paper.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11248

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
The verification tool vendors have quietly owned the hardware end of MBSE for years.

Once testing moves to hardware, the SysML model steps aside and domain-specific simulation environments take over. That handoff is where a lot of tool licensing money lives. Co-simulation platforms, transformation engines, integration middleware.

A new arXiv paper, SHIA, keeps an executable SysML model inside the verification loop directly. No intermediate transformation chains, no broker-mediated plugins, no co-simulation platform in the middle.

If that architecture matures, it eats into a layer of tooling that the industry currently treats as mandatory. That's the interesting signal. The problem everyone offloads to specialist verification tools might not need those tools at all.

Watch whether the established vendors engage with this or ignore it. That will tell you how threatened they feel.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11248

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
We built our toolchains to guarantee model drift at HIL, then blamed the drift.

The standard MBSE workflow builds a system model, then transforms it into something else for hardware verification. Every transformation is a place the model and the implementation can diverge. We added those steps ourselves, then complained about the divergence they cause.

A new arXiv paper, SHIA, removes the transformation chain and keeps the executable SysML model in the loop directly. The drift problem partly disappears because the source of drift disappears.

The community keeps trying to fix model drift with better traceability and more sync tooling. That adds more transformations to a problem caused by transformations. Removing transformations does more than managing them ever will.

The mainstream fix has been making this worse for a decade.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Your SysML model probably gets abandoned the moment verification hits hardware. A new paper argues it shouldn't.

Worth reading, worth arguing with, not yet worth betting a programme on.

## SysML v2 ISO Submission + RTFs for v2.1 Already Open (OMG/GitHub, March 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
OMG opened the v2.1 revision cycle before most teams finished migrating to v2.0.

The formal specifications got an editorial update in March 2026 for submission to ISO. At the same time, OMG established Revision Task Forces for KerML 1.1, SysML 2.1, and the Systems Modeling API & Services 1.1. No intermediate beta updates yet, but the revision clock is running.

For practitioners this is useful, not alarming. The v2.0 baseline you're migrating to is stable and heading for ISO ratification. The RTFs won't break it out from under you. They accrete resolutions into future releases.

The adoption window is fully open. Waiting for v2 to "settle" no longer works as a reason to delay. It has settled enough to be revised.

Migrate to the version that already exists rather than the frozen one that never arrives.

Source: https://github.com/systems-modeling/sysml-v2-release
#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
SysML v2 hitting ISO fast-track tells you where the standards investment is actually going.

The March 2026 editorial release submitted SysML 2.0, KerML 1.0, and the API 1.0 to ISO for ratification. Alongside that, Revision Task Forces opened for SysML 2.1, KerML 1.1, and the API 1.1. Two things at once: locking in the baseline and starting the next cycle.

That's the money signal. OMG isn't treating v2 as a finished deliverable to hand off. They're staffing continuous revision and pushing for international standardisation in parallel. That's a bet that v2 becomes the durable foundation, not a transitional format.

For teams still weighing v1 versus v2, the standards body has answered the question with its own resource allocation. The investment is flowing one direction only.

The gap now is between where the standard has committed and where practice still sits.

Source: https://github.com/systems-modeling/sysml-v2-release
#MBSE #SysML #DigitalEngineering

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
The wait-and-see position on SysML v2 migration lost its last excuse in March 2026.

The reasoning was always that v2 was too new to commit to. Fair enough in 2023. But the v2.0 spec is now submitted to ISO for ratification, and the revision task forces for v2.1 are already open. The standard has moved from "new" to "stable enough to revise."

Here's what that exposes. Standards under active use get revised continuously, so there is no frozen endpoint coming. If your migration trigger is "when it stops changing," you will never migrate. It will never stop changing. Neither did v1.

Waiting for a frozen spec was never a real strategy. It deferred a hard programme decision and called it prudence.

The version you're waiting for is the version already in front of you.

Source: https://github.com/systems-modeling/sysml-v2-release

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
SysML v2 just cleared two milestones at once, and the second one is the underreported story.

In March 2026 OMG submitted the formal specs for SysML 2.0, KerML 1.0, and the API 1.0 to ISO for fast-track ratification. At the same time, Revision Task Forces opened for SysML 2.1, KerML 1.1, and the API 1.1. Baseline locked, next cycle started.

For practitioners this signals confidence. The version you're migrating to is stable enough to standardise internationally and revise in parallel. The adoption window is fully open.

One honest complication. The RTFs haven't produced any beta updates, and ISO ratification isn't instant. So don't read this as "everything changes tomorrow." The v2.0 baseline holds while resolutions accrue for future releases. Nothing gets pulled out from under current adopters.

The takeaway is simple. The spec is stable and already improving. What's your reason for waiting now?

## DoW CTO SysML v2 Transition Guidance (cto.mil, Feb 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
US defense programmes now have CTO-level SysML v2 transition guidance to cite in a review.

In February 2026 the Systems Engineering and Architecture team within the Office of the Under Secretary of War for R&E published transition guidance to help defense acquisition programs move from SysML v1 to v2. It references DoDI 5000.97 and the interim National Defense Strategic Guidance from March 2025.

This is practical leverage. Every migration proposal I've seen stalls at "why now, and who's asking for it." A CTO-level info sheet answers both. It moves the conversation from "should we" to "how and when."

Pull the sheet, map its guidance to your programme's current SysML v1 baseline, and bring it to your next review. Concrete beats aspirational in a programme meeting.

Bring it to your next acquisition review and see how the room shifts.

Source: https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/SysML-Info-Sheet-Feb2026.pdf

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
A national defense CTO office just published v1-to-v2 transition guidance, and that turns adoption into a procurement question.

The February 2026 info sheet from the OUSW(R&E) SE&A team tells defense acquisition programs how to migrate from SysML v1 to v2, tying it to DoDI 5000.97 and digital infrastructure priorities. That's a demand signal from one of the largest buyers of systems engineering work on the planet.

Defense procurement pulls the supplier base with it. When the government frames v2 as the direction of travel, primes align, subcontractors follow, and tool requirements cascade down through the contract stack. The commercial aerospace and automotive worlds watch and adjust.

This is how a standard goes from "adopted by OMG" to "expected in your statement of work." The paperwork just started moving.

The distance between this guidance and actual programme practice is where the next two years of SE work sits.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
This defense info sheet is guidance, and programmes that read it as a mandate will hurt themselves.

The February 2026 guidance from the OUSW(R&E) team helps defense acquisition programs transition from SysML v1 to v2. It's real and it matters. But look at the framing. It offers guidance to "notionally" transition, not a directive with a deadline and a compliance clause.

The risk is programmes reading political cover into it and ripping out working v1 baselines mid-flight to look aligned. Migrating a mature v1 model on an in-flight programme carries schedule and V&V risk that a transition info sheet does not absorb for you.

Use it to justify planning and tooling investment. Don't use it to justify a disruptive migration your programme can't survive. The guidance opens the door. It doesn't push you through it.

Cite what the document says, not what you wish it said.

Source: https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/SysML-Info-Sheet-Feb2026.pdf

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
The US defense establishment just put SysML v2 transition guidance in writing. Here's what it does and doesn't do.

In February 2026 the SE&A Digital Engineering team within the Office of the Under Secretary of War for R&E published an info sheet to help defense acquisition programs transition from SysML v1 to v2. It cites DoDI 5000.97 and the March 2025 interim National Defense Strategic Guidance as the policy backing.

For defense and aerospace practitioners this is directly useful. It's a primary government source you can cite in program reviews and reference when arguing for migration budget and tooling.

Strong justification for planning. Weak justification for panic. Which is your programme treating it as?
