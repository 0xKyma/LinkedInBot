## SysFly — SysML + FMI/HiL Integration (INCOSE Webinar 203)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Your SysML model probably stops at the boundary where real testing begins. SysFly is one attempt to close that gap.

The INCOSE webinar (Ep. 203) walks through a reusable framework that connects SysML simulation to FlightGear, Dymola, and hardware-in-the-loop using FMI/FMU. The point is continuity. One architecture, carried from early design through to HiL verification, without hand-writing glue code every time the test rig changes.

Most of us have lived the alternative. The model describes the system. The test bench describes something adjacent to the system. Nobody reconciles the two until integration, and by then it costs real money.

If your models and your V&V environment still live in separate worlds, this is worth an hour of your time.

Source: https://www.incose.org/events-education/event-calendar/

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
The gap between SysML architecture and hardware verification is the last mile of MBSE, and it is finally getting attention.

FMI came out of the co-simulation world in automotive. Now aerospace practitioners are wiring it into SysML pipelines to reach HiL. SysFly (INCOSE Ep. 203) demonstrates exactly this: SysML to FMU to real-time simulation to hardware, as a reusable framework rather than a one-off.

What this signals is that the co-simulation community is quietly absorbing a problem MBSE never solved cleanly on its own. Model execution semantics inside SysML were always weak, so FMI sidesteps that by making the model talk to tools that already run.

The interesting question is whether SysML holds the source of truth, or drops to being one FMU among many.

Source: https://www.incose.org/events-education/event-calendar/

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
Reusable model-to-HiL frameworks only reuse well inside the toolchain they were built for.

SysFly is a good demonstration. SysML to FMI/FMU to Dymola and hardware, shown on an aerospace case. The framing is "reusable", and within a fixed tool set it probably is.

But the reuse claim usually falls apart on contact with a different program. Swap the simulation environment, change the fidelity requirements, introduce a supplier who models in something else, and the "reusable" framework needs a rebuild. FMI helps at the interface. It does nothing about the parameter mapping, the fidelity selection logic, or the architectural assumptions baked into the original model.

Automation at the interface is real progress. The manual effort just moved somewhere else, and the framing should say where.

Have you seen a model-to-HiL pipeline survive a second program unchanged?

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Connecting SysML models to hardware-in-the-loop without custom glue code is the demo a lot of MBSE leads have been waiting for.

The SysFly webinar (INCOSE Ep. 203) presents a model-based integration framework that uses FMI/FMU to bridge SysML simulation with FlightGear, Dymola, and HiL for aerospace development. It is pitched as reusable, meaning the same architecture flows from early design into late-stage verification.

For practitioners, that matters because the SysML-to-test handoff is where a lot of models quietly stop being useful. FMI is a sensible bridge because it is an existing, mature standard rather than another bespoke exporter.

The caution: reusability tends to hold only inside the tool set it was validated on. FMI standardises the interface, not the model content, the fidelity logic, or the parameter completeness underneath.

Worth watching if your V&V and your models still don't talk. Just test the reuse claim on your own stack.

Source: https://www.incose.org/events-education/event-calendar/

## Cris Kobryn — "SysML v2 Review: The Good, the Bad & the Ugly"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
The person who led SysML v1 gives SysML v2 a 3.5 out of 5. That should slow down anyone drafting a migration plan.

Cris Kobryn's review credits v2 for real gains in semantic rigour and interoperability. Then it lists the problems: fragmented execution semantics, immature tooling, and textual complexity. His verdict for mission-critical programs is high-risk and low-reward, at least for now.

You do not have to agree with him to use this. If you are building a v1-to-v2 transition case, walk through his failure modes and check each one against your program. If execution semantics matter to your verification approach, that is where to look hardest.

Migration timing is a real engineering decision, not a compliance box.

Source: https://sysml.org/sysml-v2/reviews/good-bad-ugly/

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
The official message is "start your SysML v2 transition." The language's original architect says the migration is high-risk and low-reward. Both cannot be casually true.

Kobryn's review runs against the DoW and INCOSE transition posture. The government guidance frames v1-to-v2 as a managed programme with transition teams and milestone maps. Kobryn frames it as premature for mission-critical work while tooling and execution semantics remain fragmented.

The distance between those two positions is the adoption gap, and it is being papered over. Standards bodies and acquisition offices move on published specs and roadmaps. Programs move on tooling that actually runs and vendors who actually support it. Those two clocks tick at different speeds.

Watch what programs do, not what the roadmaps say.

Source: https://sysml.org/sysml-v2/reviews/good-bad-ugly/

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
The consensus is that SysML v2's formal semantics are its biggest win. Kobryn argues the execution semantics are fragmented enough to be a liability. He designed v1, so this is worth taking seriously.

The community keeps treating semantic rigour as an unqualified good. A rigorous spec on paper still leaves you exposed if the execution model is inconsistent and poorly tool-supported. If different tools interpret the same construct differently, you have formalism without interoperability, which is the exact problem v2 was meant to fix.

We have seen this pattern before. A cleaner spec arrives, everyone assumes the tools will follow, and the gap between language and implementation quietly becomes the practitioner's problem.

When the person who built v1 scores v2 at 3.5, he is making a specific claim about where v2 is not finished.

#MBSE #SysML #SystemsEngineering

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
The architect of SysML v1 scored v2 at 3.5 out of 5, and the reasons are worth reading in full.

Cris Kobryn's review credits SysML v2 with genuine advances in semantic rigour, a standardised API, and better interoperability. It then flags increased abstraction, fragmented execution semantics, immature tooling, and transition risk as serious obstacles for large mission-critical programs. His overall verdict: needs significant rework.

For senior SE and MBSE leads, this matters because it comes from someone with unusual authority on the language, and it cuts against the official transition messaging.

The caution runs the other way too. This is one expert's assessment, published in February, and a 3.5 is still a passing grade. Some of his concerns are about maturity, and maturity changes with time and tooling.

Read the review, then judge your own migration timing against your own program.

Source: https://sysml.org/sysml-v2/reviews/good-bad-ugly/
