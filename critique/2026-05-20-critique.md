## C1 — "AI as Consumer and Participant: A Co-Design Agenda for MBSE Substrates and Methodology"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Your MBSE model is not a knowledge base. A new arXiv preprint makes this point precisely.

When you prompt a frontier model over your SysML artefacts, the AI reasons from its training data. Your model provides context, not ground truth. The difference matters enormously if you are treating AI outputs as derived from your authoritative model.

The paper's argument is that better prompting and better tooling cannot fix this. The model structure itself, and the methodology that governs how engineers build it, must change to support AI participation from the start. That co-design has not happened yet on any programme I am aware of.

If you are already deploying AI over your MBSE models, ask yourself: is the AI retrieving from your model, or is it pattern-matching against aerospace documentation it saw during training? Those are very different things.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 2 — The adjacent threat

Software engineering already has this problem solved, at least partially. Retrieval-augmented generation over structured codebases is a production capability. MBSE does not have an equivalent.

A preprint published last month argues that nobody designed MBSE model structures for AI consumption, and attaching AI tooling to them does not change that. The implication is that if the MBSE community does not redesign its substrates deliberately, the software engineering community will define the standard for how AI reasons over system models, because they are already ahead.

That is not a comfortable position for a discipline that has spent a decade arguing that models should be the authoritative source of truth.

The money in AI-assisted engineering is following the software toolchain right now. MBSE needs a concrete answer to this, not a roadmap.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25526

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Adding AI to your MBSE programme right now is mostly theatre.

A paper from late April argues that well-prompted frontier models produce coherent output over SysML models, but the reasoning comes from training, not from the model. Your carefully governed, change-controlled system model is functioning as an elaborate prompt.

The community response to this has been to build better AI interfaces to existing models. That response treats the model structure as sound and the tooling as the thing that needs to catch up. The paper argues that the methodology is what needs to change, and layering tooling on top of a flawed methodology produces confident-sounding outputs with no real grounding in your model.

We have seen this before. We built dashboards over poorly structured models and called it traceability. This is the same pattern, faster.

## C2 — "Uncertainty Modeling for SysML v2" (PSUM-SysMLv2)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

SysML v2 shipped without native uncertainty constructs. A published extension now fills that gap.

The PSUM-SysMLv2 profile integrates the OMG's Probability and Statistics for Uncertainty Modeling (PSUM) metamodel directly into SysML v2. It gives you formally defined semantics for specifying indeterminacy sources and propagating uncertainty through a system model. The authors validated it across seven case studies and published the profile openly.

For anyone modelling autonomous systems, cyber-physical systems, or anything with probabilistic performance requirements, this is immediately usable. You do not need to wait for the SysML v2.1 RTF to address this in the core spec.

The gap between what SysML v2 formalised and what defence and space programmes actually need to model has always been wider than the spec committees acknowledge. This paper closes one part of it.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

#SysML #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 2 — The adoption gap

The SysML v2 specification promised rigorous, computable system models. Then it shipped without uncertainty representation.

A February preprint proposes a systematic extension using the PSUM metamodel, validated across seven case studies. The profile is open source. The capability is real.

But the adoption problem is layered: SysML v2 tooling is still catching up to the core spec. A community-developed profile extending a spec that tools have not fully implemented yet sits several layers of adoption friction away from a practitioner actually using it on a programme.

The uncertainty modelling capability exists. The pathway from "published extension" to "supported in Cameo or Capella or the Pilot Implementations" is not short. The gap between what the research community produces and what gets into production tooling remains the single biggest drag on MBSE methodology advancement.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

#SysML #MBSE #DigitalEngineering

---

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Seven validated case studies for uncertainty modelling in SysML v2, and the MBSE community will largely ignore it.

The PSUM-SysMLv2 paper is technically solid. The extension is formally grounded, the case studies cover real problem types, and the authors made the profile openly available. On paper, this is exactly what the community asked for when it complained that SysML v2 lacked uncertainty constructs.

But the programmes that most need uncertainty-aware models, space, autonomous systems, hypersonics, are also the programmes furthest behind on SysML v2 adoption. They are still fighting the model governance and tooling maturity battles that v2 was supposed to resolve.

Publishing a rigorous extension to a language that most target programmes have not yet adopted does not move the problem. We keep building the roof before the foundation is in place.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21641

## C3 — "MBSE Co-Pilot: A Research Roadmap" (Zhang et al., *Systems Engineering*, 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

Any vendor pitching "AI-assisted MBSE" right now should have to answer for this paper first.

Zhang et al. publish a structured research roadmap for an MBSE Co-Pilot in the INCOSE *Systems Engineering* journal. What makes it useful is not the AI framing. It is the explicit critique buried in the setup: organisations that treat MBSE as a one-size-fits-all solution overload models with excessive detail, and that increases programme complexity rather than reducing it.

An AI layer on top of an overloaded, poorly scoped model does not fix the overload. It automates the confusion.

The roadmap defines capability levels from basic query support up to autonomous model evolution. That structure is worth having when you are evaluating what a vendor actually means when they say "Co-Pilot."

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70011

### World Event Option 1 — Requirements Volatility and Verification Gap Frame

The US Navy doubled its shipbuilding budget over 20 years and ended up with no more ships than it had in 2003.

The Navy's own 2026 shipbuilding plan names the cause directly: requirements expanded during execution, mature designs were modified after programme start, and cost and schedule estimates were chronically optimistic. That is not a funding story. That is a requirements volatility and unstable baseline story at a scale most of us will never encounter in a single programme.

The prescribed fix is also an SE move. Shifting from monolithic single-yard builds toward distributed, modular production is an explicit attempt to reduce coupling in the production system. Whether it works depends entirely on how well the interfaces between those distributed sites are defined and controlled.

Systems engineers fight this failure mode on programmes worth millions. The Navy just documented it on one worth hundreds of billions.

Source: https://news.usni.org/2026/05/11/u-s-navys-2026-shipbuilding-plan

### World Event Option 2 — Feedback Loops and Unintended Consequences Frame

Spending more on a system does not fix the system if the system's design is wrong.

The US Navy's 2026 shipbuilding plan acknowledges that its acquisition system grew administratively while the fleet it was meant to produce did not grow at all. Budget doubled. Ships did not. The acquisition process performed exactly as designed. The design was wrong for the actual output required.

This is a feedback loop problem. Requirements expanded mid-execution. Costs compounded. Schedules slipped. Each programme absorbed the overrun and moved on, so no single failure triggered a correction signal strong enough to change acquisition behaviour across the portfolio. The balancing loop that should have fired — fewer ships delivered, so change the process — never closed.

When a system's feedback mechanisms only measure inputs and not outcomes, you can optimise for decades and still move backwards.

Source: https://news.usni.org/2026/05/11/u-s-navys-2026-shipbuilding-plan
