## QSysMM/QSysML — SysML v2 Extended for Quantum Systems

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Model transformations can silently delete superposition and entanglement, and nothing in your tool will flag it.

That is the core claim in a new paper from Siyuan Ji and colleagues at Loughborough. They propose QSysMM, a framework for managing models of engineered quantum systems, and within it QSysML, a modelling language built on the SysML v2 stack.

The failure mode matters even if you never touch quantum. Structural checks confirm a transformation is valid. They say nothing about whether the physics survived the trip.

If you are integrating a quantum sensor or clock into a larger architecture, this becomes a design-review question now. Ask what your transformation actually preserves, not just what it validates.

The paper is conceptual. No tooling yet. But the gap it names is real and worth watching.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10347

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (the adjacent threat)
Quantum systems engineering is about to inherit every modelling problem we never fully solved, plus a few we cannot see.

A new Loughborough paper proposes QSysML on the SysML v2 stack, aimed at managing models of engineered quantum systems. The interesting part is why they needed it. Classical model transformation can destroy quantum properties without raising any error, because structural correctness and physical correctness are not the same check.

This is the pattern when a new domain lands on old infrastructure. The infrastructure holds, right up until the assumptions underneath it stop being true.

Defence and space programmes are already funding quantum sensing and timing. The modelling methodology is lagging the hardware, and that gap will not close on its own.

Who owns quantum model integrity on your programme right now?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10347

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
Extending SysML v2 for quantum systems solves the easy half of the problem and hides the hard half.

The new QSysML proposal builds on the SysML v2 stack, and the reasoning is pragmatic: integrate with existing classical workflows rather than start from scratch. I understand the appeal. Adoption is easier when engineers keep their tools.

But the paper's own finding cuts against that choice. Model transformations silently strip superposition and entanglement, and the transformation machinery is exactly what you inherit when you build on the existing stack.

Stapling quantum semantics onto a language designed for classical systems means you carry forward the assumptions that caused the loss in the first place. Compatibility and correctness are pulling in different directions here.

Build on v2 for adoption, or break from it for fidelity. This paper wants both, and I am not convinced you can have them.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10347

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
A new paper argues SysML v2 can quietly break the systems it is meant to model.

Siyuan Ji and colleagues at Loughborough propose QSysMM, a framework for managing models of engineered quantum systems, structured around four concerns: ontological, abstraction, composition, and exposure. Inside it sits QSysML, a modelling language built on the SysML v2 technology stack.

The finding worth your attention: model transformations in quantum systems can remove properties like superposition and entanglement with no error detection at all. Current synchronisation methods do not catch it.

So the takeaway is a warning as much as a proposal. If you touch quantum subsystems, transformation integrity is now a fidelity problem, not just a structural one.

But this is early work. No tooling, no case study, and building on v2 carries the very transformation model that caused the loss. Promising direction, open questions.

Is v2's extension model the right vehicle for this, or the wrong one?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10347

## INCOSE MBSE Roadmap — Bibliometric Review (Velthuizen et al., 2026)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
INCOSE said MBSE would be the standard way of working by 2020. A new bibliometric review of the roadmap shows where that landed.

Velthuizen and colleagues mapped MBSE research against INCOSE's roughly 15-year roadmap. The gaps they name are not obscure. MBSE theories, standardised methodologies, MBSE standards, MBSE metrics, integration with hardware and software models, and tool interoperability all still need substantial work.

Read that list as a practitioner and it stings, because those are the exact things that make MBSE hard on a real programme. Interoperability and metrics are where I lose the most time.

The roadmap was ambitious. The research followed some parts and skipped others.

Which of these gaps is costing you the most right now?

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70030

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (the adoption gap)
Six years past INCOSE's 2020 deadline, MBSE still is not the standard way of working, and a new review shows why.

Velthuizen and colleagues ran a bibliometric analysis of the INCOSE MBSE roadmap. The research community moved on some fronts and left others thin. MBSE metrics, tool interoperability, and underlying theory remain open.

That distribution is telling. We invested heavily in tooling and languages, which are visible and vendor-fundable. We invested less in metrics and interoperability, which are shared costs nobody owns.

The adoption gap between MBSE the promise and MBSE the daily practice sits almost exactly on the areas the research underfunded. Capability without measurement or integration does not become standard practice. It stays a specialist activity.

The roadmap was not wrong about the destination. We just skipped the unglamorous parts.

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70030

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
The MBSE community keeps blaming tooling for slow adoption. This review points somewhere less comfortable.

Velthuizen and colleagues found that after a 15-year roadmap, MBSE still lacks agreed theory, standardised methodologies, and metrics. We talk endlessly about SysML v1 versus v2, tool licences, and API maturity. We talk far less about whether we can prove MBSE works.

Without metrics you cannot show a programme manager that modelling paid off. Without theory and shared methodology, every organisation reinvents its own practice and calls it best. That is the conversation the community avoids, because tooling is easier to argue about than results.

Better tools will not fix a discipline that cannot measure its own value.

We spent 15 years asking which tool. We should have spent more asking whether it worked.

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70030

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
INCOSE promised MBSE would be standard practice by 2020. A new review checks the receipts.

Velthuizen and colleagues published a bibliometric review of the INCOSE MBSE roadmap in the INCOSE Systems Engineering journal. They mapped where research actually went across the roadmap's roughly 15-year span, against INCOSE's stated vision of MBSE as the standard way of working within SE.

The result: several core areas still need substantial research. MBSE theories, standardised methodologies, standards, metrics, hardware and software integration, and tool interoperability all remain open.

For a senior audience that lived through this period, that is validation of a suspicion many of us held. Progress was real, but uneven.

One caution. A bibliometric review counts papers, not practice. Thin research on metrics does not automatically mean thin capability in industry, though the two usually track.

Did the roadmap fail, or did we just follow the easy parts of it?

Source: https://incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70030
