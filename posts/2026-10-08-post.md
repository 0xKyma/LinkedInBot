## SysON/SysIDE Syntax Incompatibility with OMG SysML v2 Release

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
If you imported OMG SysML v2 example files into SysON recently, check your element counts before you trust them.

SysON's bundled SysIDE CLI 0.9.0 rejects 33 files from the OMG's 2026-08 release. The Pilot Implementation parses them fine. SysON does not.

The failure mode is the problem. In v2026.9.0, Annex A imported as 194 elements out of 1,581 lines, and the upload reported success. The file truncates at the first unsupported construct and nothing tells you.

The patched v2026.9.2 adds a warning, but the model still imports truncated. You get no line number and no construct name. You have to diff against the source to find what you lost.

So verify your imports by hand for now. Count elements. Compare against the original. A green checkmark is not evidence your model is complete.

Source: github.com/eclipse-syson/syson/issues/2543

Open-source SysML v2 tooling cannot reliably round-trip the OMG's own release files. That is the gap between the spec and practice right now.

SysON's bundled parser rejects 33 files from the official 2026-08 release, including Annex A. The OMG Pilot Implementation reads them without error. Two reference-adjacent tools disagree on what valid SysML v2 looks like.

The spec being published does not make the ecosystem interoperable. Each tool implements the grammar at its own pace, and the laggards fail quietly instead of loudly. That is what keeps capability and practice apart.

For anyone planning a v2 migration, this is the real signal. The language is standardised. The implementations are not aligned yet. Interop between tools is still an open question, not a solved one.

Treat tool compatibility as something to test on your actual models, not something to assume.

## DE ROI Navy Case Study — INCOSE Systems Engineering (Shefa & Topcu)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
A new INCOSE journal paper puts a number on Digital Engineering ROI: a 50.1% reduction in median project duration.

Shefa and Topcu studied a Navy SE team doing preliminary design for post-production changes to legacy assets. Mixed methods, single program office. They estimate DE transformation could cut median duration in half and reduce the standard deviation by 41.5%.

If you have been asked to justify DE spend without data, this is the first peer-reviewed quantitative case I can point to. The variance reduction matters as much as the median. Predictable schedules are often worth more to a programme than fast ones.

Read it before you cite it. One program office is not every program office, and sustainment work is not greenfield development. But it is a real baseline to argue from instead of anecdotes.

Source: incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70092

#DigitalEngineering #MBSE #SystemsEngineering

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (the money signal)
INCOSE and the DoD have promoted Digital Engineering for years on mostly anecdotal benefits. A new paper finally attaches a figure to the claim.

Shefa and Topcu, in the INCOSE Systems Engineering journal, report what they call the first quantitative evidence of DE's ROI: a 50.1% median schedule reduction in a Navy sustainment case. That the authors can credibly claim "first" tells you something about where the evidence base has been.

Investment has flowed toward DE faster than the measurement of it. Programmes committed budgets on promise, not on numbers. A study like this is what the funding has been waiting for, and what the next round of funding will cite.

Expect this paper to appear in acquisition briefings regardless of its sample size. One number travels further than a hundred case studies without one.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
A 50.1% median schedule reduction from one program office is not evidence that DE halves schedules. It is a hypothesis with a decimal point.

The Shefa and Topcu paper is genuinely valuable, and I'm glad someone finally measured something. But a single Navy sustainment case cannot carry the weight that number is about to be asked to carry. Watch how fast the "50%" gets quoted without the context around it.

The risk is familiar. One strong result becomes a slide, the slide becomes a target, and the target gets applied to programmes that look nothing like the one studied. Post-production changes to legacy assets are a specific kind of work. Generalising from it is a choice, not a given.

Use the paper. Cite the method, not just the headline. And resist turning a careful case study into a universal promise.

Source: incose.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sys.70092

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown

The complication is scope. This is one program office and one type of work. The authors themselves frame it as first evidence, not settled fact, so generalising to other programmes is unproven.

Strong study, specific context. Cite the method with the number, not the number alone.
