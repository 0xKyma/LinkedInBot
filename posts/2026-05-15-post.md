## SysML 2.1 RTF — Already Revising v2.0 Before Anyone Has Adopted It

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
The OMG has established Revision Task Forces for KerML 1.1, SysML 2.1, and the Systems Modeling API 1.1. No resolutions approved yet, but the revision machinery is already running.

Tool vendors have not completed v2.0 conformance suites. Most organisations are still mid-transition from v1, or haven't started. And the spec is already moving.

For anyone building a v2 migration roadmap right now, this matters. The tooling you're evaluating is targeting a spec that will change. The training you're designing may need revision before your programme completes it.

Document a version assumption explicitly. Pick a point in the spec's current state, name it, and build your roadmap against that named version.

What version are you designing your v2 transition plan against?

### Option 2 — The adoption gap
The OMG has already established RTFs for SysML 2.1, KerML 1.1, and API 1.1. Most tools have not reached v2.0 conformance. The spec writers have moved on.

This is the adoption gap, and the standards body's own timeline makes it visible. Organisations are still working out whether their tools can handle v2.0. Meanwhile, contributors with institutional knowledge are already focused on the next revision cycle.

Volunteer cycles and committee momentum do not wait for practitioner readiness. That is not a criticism of the process. It is just how standards bodies function.

Organisations that treat the published spec as stable ground, without a documented version-risk assumption, will carry that debt into their programmes.

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
Every SE forum recommends adopting SysML v2 now. The OMG establishing RTFs for v2.1 before v2.0 has meaningful tool coverage makes that advice harder to defend.

The argument for early adoption is usually about building capability before the field matures. That argument assumes the spec is stable enough to build on. A revision task force with open resolutions is not a stable foundation.

Early adoption of v1 taught the community something: tooling divergence during a spec's early life creates migration debt that programmes carry for years. The same dynamic is setting up again.

Go in with a documented version-risk assumption, or accept that the debt is coming regardless.

## IEEE: "Enhancing MBSE via Graph-Theoretic Partitioning and System Model Metrics"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Two of the hardest questions in an MBSE architecture review: is this model well-structured, and how would you know?

This IEEE paper offers a concrete method. The authors convert system functions and interfaces into a functional dependence graph, apply community detection, and derive three metrics: system complexity, system modularity, and system test effort. They validated the approach on an autonomous mobile robot and two automotive propulsion systems.

These metrics come directly from the model. You do not need a senior architect to eyeball the decomposition, or a consensus that it "feels right."

That does not replace engineering judgement. It gives you something to argue with instead of arguing about whether to argue.

If your programme ran these metrics on your current architecture model, what would they tell you?

Source: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11224723/

### Option 2 — The displacement angle
Architecture quality in MBSE has historically relied on review boards, experienced architects, and documented heuristics. This IEEE paper starts replacing that with computable structural metrics derived directly from the model graph.

That displacement matters for how programmes staff and govern architecture work. When the model itself computes complexity, modularity, and test effort, the architecture review process changes. The question shifts from "is this decomposition reasonable?" to "what thresholds trigger a review in the first place?"

Experienced architects still matter. But where they apply that experience shifts. And in programmes where senior SE capacity runs thin, that shift has real resourcing implications.

Which tools integrate these metrics first will determine a lot about what the others have to catch up to.

Source: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11224723/

#MBSE #DigitalEngineering #SystemsEngineering

---

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
Computable MBSE model metrics sound like exactly what the community has been asking for. My concern is that optimising for measurable structure produces different outcomes than building a good architecture.

Community detection on a functional dependence graph will reward clean decomposition and penalise coupling. Those are real quality signals. But some of the best systems architectures couple tightly in exactly the right places because the physics demands it. A metric derived from graph topology does not know that.

The risk is that teams start designing models to score well rather than to reflect the system accurately. Every other metric-driven discipline has produced that failure mode.

Use these metrics as diagnostic tools, not targets.
