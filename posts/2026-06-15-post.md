## INCOSE IS 2026 (Yokohama) + SysML v2 Usage Modelling

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
SysML v1 taught us to start from structure. Blocks first, then parts, then behaviour bolted on later.

INCOSE IS 2026 opens in Yokohama this week. Wylie & Liddy presented a paper on usage modelling in SysML v2 as a primary lens. That means modelling how the system gets used before you commit to a decomposition.

This matters for anyone picking up SysML v2. The language makes usage a first-class concept. But most of us will carry over our part-first instincts and never notice we are doing it.

Try this on your next model. Define the usages before the definitions. See whether your structure comes out different.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Usage-first modelling in SysML v2 sounds like a small choice. It changes the whole shape of your model.

INCOSE IS 2026 opens in Yokohama this week. One accepted paper (Wylie & Liddy) explores usage modelling in SysML v2 as a primary lens, and a separate panel covers current challenges in leading digital engineering.

The takeaway for practitioners: SysML v2 makes usage a first-class construct, so you can model how a system is exercised before fixing its decomposition. That flips the typical v1 workflow.

The caution is that usage-first is a discipline, not a feature. Teams carrying part-first habits will use v2 syntax to build v1 structures and miss the benefit entirely. The tool won't stop you.

If you are evaluating v2, the method question matters more than the tool question. Which are you spending time on?

## NDIA/INCOSE/IEEE 2026 MBSE Symposium (Huntsville)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
"Toward an Empirical Foundation Through Digital Engineering" is a paper title that should make us all squirm a little.

The NDIA MBSE Symposium in Huntsville ran it last week. The framing implies what most of us suspect: a lot of our DE claims rest on belief, not evidence. We say modelling reduces rework. We say it catches integration errors early. How often do we measure it?

For practitioners, this is a prompt. On your current programme, what DE outcome can you actually point to with data? Not a story about a defect you caught. A number.

What is the one DE metric your programme tracks that you trust?

Source: https://www.ndiatennvalley.org/2026MBSE

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
Agentic AI in MBSE workflows moved from speculation to conference agenda in about a year.

The NDIA MBSE Symposium last week in Huntsville included "Extending AI-Assisted MBSE for Agentic Software Synthesis." Not AI as a chatbot helping you write requirements. AI agents generating software from models with limited human steering.

This shows where the money is going. Defence programmes now want the model to drive downstream automation, not just carry information between people. Once that takes hold, a model earns its value through how machine-actionable it is.

That raises the bar on model quality. Humans tolerate loose, inconsistent models because they can fill the gaps. Agents can't. They act on exactly what you wrote.

A model built for an agent carries far more rigour than the ones we draw today.

Source: https://www.ndiatennvalley.org/2026MBSE

#MBSE #DigitalEngineering #AI

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
A symposium paper just admitted in its title what the community has dodged for fifteen years.

"Systems Engineering 2.0: Toward an Empirical Foundation Through Digital Engineering" ran at NDIA Huntsville last week. The word that matters is empirical. It concedes that our current foundation rests on faith.

We avoid this conversation because the answer might sting. If we measured DE outcomes rigorously across programmes, some would show no improvement at all. Some might show the modelling effort cost more than it returned. Nobody wants to be the programme that produced that number.

But faith doesn't survive a budget review
