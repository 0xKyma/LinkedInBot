## Models as Governed Interfaces for AI-Native MBSE (arXiv:2609.16252)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Your SysML v2 model is machine-readable. That does not mean it is ready for an AI to read it.

A new paper accepted at MODELS 2026 makes a point I have not seen stated this cleanly. When an AI queries a structurally complete model and hits a gap in derivation, provenance, or status, it does not stop. It fills the gap from training data. That answer looks confident and cites nothing you can trace.

The authors call the missing property "epistemic adequacy". In practice it means your model needs to carry derivation chains, provenance, and status tags before you point any agent at it.

So the work is not just modelling the system. It is tagging what you know, how you know it, and how confident you are.

Go audit one of your models. How much of it could an AI actually justify reading?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16252

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
Everyone is racing to connect LLMs to SysML v2 models. The gap between "the model is queryable" and "the model is trustworthy to query" is wider than the tooling admits.

A paper out of Loughborough, accepted at MODELS 2026, frames this as two separate problems. Read-side adequacy: can the model answer a query with derivation, status, and provenance instead of inviting a guess. Write-side admissibility: can you gate an AI contribution before it enters the record.

The money is flowing into the read side right now. Vendors are shipping API access and chat interfaces. Almost nobody is building the write-side gate.

That imbalance matters. An agent that can write to your model without admissibility control is a liability, not an assistant. The governance layer is the harder engineering problem and the one getting the least attention.

Which vendor ships a real write-side gate first?

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
SysML v2 machine-readability was sold as the thing that unlocks AI in MBSE. It solves the wrong half of the problem.

The pitch has been consistent for two years. Once models are programmatically accessible, agents can read them, reason over them, and contribute. The spec landed. The APIs shipped. And a paper accessed at MODELS 2026 shows why that was never enough.

A structurally complete model still lacks derivation chains, provenance, and status tags. An AI reading it does not abstain when it hits those gaps. It guesses from training data, and the guess is neither verifiable nor governed.

So the readability we celebrated makes the failure mode worse, because the output looks authoritative. We built the pipe and forgot the water needs to be clean.

Machine-readable is not the same as machine-trustworthy. Until we tag what our models actually know, AI participation is confident hallucination with a SysML wrapper.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Machine-readable SysML v2 does not make a model AI-ready. That is the claim, and it holds up.

The paper, from Gower, Henshaw and Ji at Loughborough, is accepted at MODELS 2026. It argues that AI participation in MBSE needs a property beyond structural completeness, which they call epistemic adequacy: derivation, provenance, and status tagging. They split it into read-side adequacy and write-side admissibility, and they test the idea against the public Apollo 11 SysML v2 reconstruction.

Why it matters: if you are pointing any agent at your model, this names the exact gaps that produce confident, ungoverned answers.

The caution: it is a New Ideas paper. The framework is conceptual and there is no implemented tooling yet. You cannot buy this, only design toward it.

Worth reading before you wire an LLM into your model store. What would epistemic adequacy cost you to add to an existing model?

## Automated SysML-Based Verification of Discipline-Specific Models (arXiv:2606.29006)

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
If your SysML verification lives entirely in parametric diagrams, you are checking performance and ignoring behaviour.

A paper from MBDA Systems and Loughborough names this directly. Most SysML-based verification does two things badly. It gets built on tool-specific proprietary APIs, so it does not port. And it focuses on parametric properties, so behavioural and interface properties go unverified.

Ordering. Timing. State-based responses. These are the properties that actually break integration, and they sit outside the parametric world most of us default to.

Their answer uses the UML Testing Profile and runs across two independent toolchains to prove it is not locked to one vendor. That portability alone is worth the read.

Go look at your last verification model. How many of your interface behaviours were actually verified, and how many were assumed?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29006

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
Proprietary verification APIs are quietly locking programmes into single vendors, and almost nobody prices that in.

A paper from MBDA Systems and Loughborough puts numbers behind a discomfort a lot of us have felt. SysML verification approaches get built on tool-specific APIs. When you switch tools, or run a multi-vendor programme, the verification logic does not come with you. You rebuild it.

Their alternative uses the UML Testing Profile and is demonstrated across two independent toolchains. The point is portability. Verification that survives a tool migration.

This is the transition risk nobody discusses when a programme commits to a SysML platform. The models might be portable one day through the standard API. The verification logic built on proprietary extensions will not be.

Tool-agnostic verification is going to matter more as SysML v2 forces migration decisions. Is your V&V work portable, or is it hostage to your current tool?

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
Parametric diagrams have been the face of SysML verification for years. They verify the least important half of the system.

We got comfortable. Parametrics handle performance properties cleanly, they demo well, and they give you a satisfying pass or fail. So verification effort concentrated there. A paper from MBDA Systems and Loughborough says that concentration is a blind spot.

Performance is rarely what breaks at integration. Ordering breaks. Timing breaks. State-based responses break. Those are behavioural and interface properties, and the parametric-first habit leaves them unverified.

We convinced ourselves that a model full of green parametric checks was a verified model. It was a model with verified equations and unverified behaviour.

The paper demonstrates behavioural and interface verification across two toolchains using the UML Testing Profile. It is a direct challenge to how most of us have scoped V&V.

Parametric-only verification is a comfortable habit that verifies the wrong things.

### Option 4 — Balanced breakdown
Most SysML verification is tool-locked and parametric-only. This paper argues both are fixable, and shows how.

The work, from Marley at MBDA Systems and Ji at Loughborough, diagnoses two systemic flaws in current practice: verification built on proprietary APIs that will not port, and a focus on parametric properties that skips behaviour and interfaces. Their process uses the UML Testing Profile and gets demonstrated end-to-end across two independent SysML toolchains.

Why it matters: it covers ordering, timing, and state-based responses, the behavioural properties that break real integrations. And the two-toolchain demonstration is genuine evidence of portability.

The caution: the stakeholder needs were derived from interviews and literature, so the validation is early. Two toolchains is a proof of concept, not a fleet-wide guarantee.

Still, this is one of the more directly applicable SysML papers I have read this year. Would a tool-agnostic verification process change how your programme scopes V&V?
