## OMG SysML v2 Certification Programme Launch

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

OMG just launched a formal SysML v2 certification. If you hold an OCSMP, you should pay attention.

The new "SysML v2 Model User" credential went live on June 2. A "Model Builder Fundamental" tier follows in early 2027. OMG is positioning itself as the authoritative certifying body, given it owns the spec.

The OCSMP covered SysML v1. It doesn't map cleanly to v2. So practitioners now face a real question: do you let your v1 credential speak for itself, pursue the new cert, or wait for the Builder tier before committing?

My read: the Model User tier is a low bar. If procurement language starts requiring SysML v2 proficiency, a Model User credential probably satisfies it on paper. Whether it reflects actual modelling competence is a separate matter entirely.

What criteria is your organisation using to decide whether this credential is worth pursuing?

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle

Certifying SysML v2 proficiency before the tooling is mature enough to support it in production is backwards sequencing.

OMG launched its "Model User" credential on June 2. The enthusiasm is understandable. But what exactly are we certifying? The ability to read and write a language that current tools only partially implement? ESA published a peer-reviewed pilot this year confirming that mature SysML v2 tool support doesn't yet exist at production scale.

A credential tied to a spec is only useful if the spec and its tooling are stable enough to train against consistently. Right now, organisations are still figuring out what v2 even means in practice. Certifying people ahead of that stabilisation doesn't accelerate the transition. It risks producing a cohort whose training is obsolete by the time the tooling catches up.

The Builder tier, backed by mature tool coverage, would be a more credible starting point for treating this as a workforce quality signal.

## ESA "Paving the Way for SysML v2" Pilot Study

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle

ESA ran a formal pilot to migrate its MBSE methodology from SysML v1 to v2. The finding that deserves your attention: current tooling is not mature enough to support it in production.

The paper, published in INCOSE's Systems Engineering journal in February, documents ESA's iterative process of mapping its established v1-based methodology concepts to SysML v2 elements. To do the work at all, the team had to develop their own textual notation language extension and test it on a reference space mission model.

That's a significant constraint. ESA has deep modelling expertise and the resources to build workarounds. Most programmes don't. If ESA needs custom extensions to pilot v2 at a case-study level, organisations expecting to transition production programmes this year should be recalibrating their timelines.

Read the paper before you commit your programme to a v2 migration schedule.

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle (The adoption gap)

ESA published a peer-reviewed v1-to-v2 migration pilot. The gap between spec maturity and tool readiness is wider than most transition roadmaps acknowledge.

The paper by Duroy et al. in INCOSE's Systems Engineering journal documents what happens when a major space agency with a mature, well-documented v1 methodology tries to systematically map its concepts to v2. The language concepts mostly translate. The tooling does not yet support the result.

ESA built a textual notation language extension to proceed at all. That's not a workaround most programme teams can replicate. And ESA's methodology covers numerous space and non-space projects, so this wasn't a small-scale experiment.

Organisations announcing v2 transitions right now are largely transitioning on paper. Tooling is the constraint, and no amount of methodology guidance closes that gap.

The community keeps producing guidance on how to think about v2 transitions. Methodology frameworks. Training pathways. Now a certification programme. But a practitioner team that completes all of that and then opens their modelling tool will hit the same wall ESA hit: partial implementation, missing features, no mature v2 environment to work in.

Trained engineers waiting for software to catch up does not constitute an accelerated transition.
