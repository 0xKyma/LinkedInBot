## Xames & Topcu — "Digital Engineering Transformation as a Sociotechnical Challenge"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Buying tools does not give you a digital engineering capability. A new INCOSE journal paper puts numbers on why.

Xames and Topcu catalogued 49 barriers to DE transformation and sorted them across six sociotechnical dimensions. Workforce readiness, leadership support, and cultural alignment show up again and again. The technical barriers matter, but they are not the ones that stall programmes.

If you have run a DE rollout, this will feel familiar. The model repository stands up fine. The problem is the engineers who never open it, the leads who still review PDFs, and the reviews that ignore the authoritative source.

So before your next tool procurement, ask who is going to change how they work, and whether anyone above you will back that change when it gets uncomfortable.

Which barrier has stalled your DE work the most?

## Jézégou et al. — "Evaluating MBSE Approaches and Tools for Aircraft Design and Certification"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
Papyrus prototypes faster. It also breaks faster when the regulation text changes. A new comparative study puts that trade-off on the table for aircraft certification.

Jézégou and colleagues ran two SysML toolchains head to head for EASA CS-23 workflows: Eclipse Papyrus with MATLAB against CATIA Magic MSoSA. Papyrus got them to a working prototype quickly. But it leaned on text-pattern parsing that proved fragile when the regulatory wording shifted. MSoSA gave more integrated execution and less dependence on parsing, at the cost of licensing and integration effort.

If you are picking a toolchain for a certification programme, this is the trade-off you are actually making. Speed to prototype versus resilience to change. Neither is free.

So the real decision is how often your regulatory baseline moves, and whether your team can absorb the rework when it does.

Which side of that trade-off has burned you before?

## arXiv:2608.09547 — "MBSE Framework for SysML-Driven Design of Autonomous UAVs"

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
A new paper maps SysML constructs straight onto ROS 2, block by block. If you build autonomous systems, that mapping is the useful part.

The authors propose a four-layer framework: stakeholder requirements, functional decomposition, logical architecture, then physical and software allocation. The part worth stealing is the traceable mapping. SysML blocks become ROS 2 nodes, flow ports become topics, request-response interactions become services, and goal-oriented behaviours become actions.

That is a concrete way to keep your architecture model connected to the software that actually runs. Most MBSE efforts on autonomous programmes lose the thread right there, at the handoff from model to code.

The framework uses SysML v1 and the full diagram suite, so nothing here needs new tooling.

If you work autonomous systems, would this mapping survive contact with your codebase?

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09547

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
The gap that keeps stalling autonomous programmes is the model-to-code handoff. This paper attacks it directly with a SysML-to-ROS 2 mapping.

Autonomous systems are software-heavy, and ROS 2 is where much of that software lives. A four-layer SysML framework that maps blocks to nodes, ports to topics, and behaviours to actions is an attempt to bridge the systems model and the running software.

That adjacency matters. The robotics and software world has its own architecture conventions, and they have not waited for MBSE to catch up. If systems engineers want to stay relevant on autonomous programmes, the model has to connect to the software architecture people already use.

Frameworks like this are how MBSE earns its place in a ROS-centric development flow, rather than sitting beside it as documentation.

Does your systems model connect to your software architecture, or run parallel to it?

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
This framework maps SysML v1 onto ROS 2, and that choice is where I would push back.

The four-layer approach is sound and the SysML-to-ROS 2 mapping is genuinely useful. But SysML v1 semantics are loose, and mapping loose semantics onto a precise software architecture is where these efforts usually leak. Two engineers read the same v1 model differently, and the ROS 2 mapping inherits that ambiguity.

This is exactly the problem SysML v2's formal semantics were built to address. A mapping to ROS 2 from a model with defined semantics has a real shot at being tool-checkable. A mapping from v1 depends on convention and discipline holding across the team.

The framework is a good demonstration. Built on v1, it demonstrates the limit as much as the capability.

Would this mapping be more trustworthy on SysML v2?
