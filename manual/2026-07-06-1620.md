## Eye Surgery Lens Ordering Automation

### Option 1 — Practitioner angle
The AI was the smallest part of the eye surgery automation I built.

The system takes surgical theatre lists from their practice management software and produces supplier-ready lens order forms for both Alcon and Johnson & Johnson, plus a colour-coded sheet for a human to check.

Ordering these lenses is manual and error-prone. Staff hand-copy lens type, power, eye, and quantity into supplier forms. Surgeons write names inconsistently. A wrong power creates clinical and financial risk.

So safety drove every decision. Redaction strips patient names, dates of birth, and Medicare numbers before anything reaches the model. Claude must call one structured tool, so every order returns as clean typed JSON. Fuzzy matching corrects messy lens names against a canonical catalogue. A rules engine tags each order GREEN, REVIEW, or HOLD.

Staff confirm anything flagged. The system never orders blindly.

AI works best when you structure it, check it, and keep it on a leash.

### Option 2 — Industry/trend angle
Everyone wants to bolt an LLM onto a workflow. Almost no one designs the guardrails first.

I built a lens ordering system for an eye surgery practice. It converts theatre lists into supplier-ready order forms for Alcon and Johnson & Johnson, with a validation sheet for a human to sign off.

The model does one job: extraction. Everything else is deterministic. Redaction runs first, so the AI never sees patient identity. Fuzzy matching maps messy lens names to a canonical catalogue. A rules engine flags anything risky before a human reviews it.

This is where automation in regulated domains is heading. The AI handles the fuzzy, ambiguous middle. Deterministic logic handles anything that can hurt someone. The interesting engineering sits in that boundary.

Vendors sell the model. The value lives in the system around it.

Which part of your workflow actually needs the model, and which part just needs good rules?

### Option 3 — Contrarian angle
"Human in the loop" is doing a lot of work in most AI safety claims. Usually it means a tired person rubber-stamping output they can't verify.

I built a lens ordering system for an eye surgery practice. A wrong lens power carries clinical and financial risk, so a human has to catch mistakes. But a reviewer scanning raw AI output will miss things. People trust clean-looking text.

So I designed the review to fight that. A rules engine tags every order GREEN, REVIEW, or HOLD with plain flags like "toric grade missing" or "confirm negative power with surgeon." Staff only scrutinise what the system already suspects.

The human stays in the loop. But the loop tells them where to look.

A human in the loop only helps if the system directs their attention. Otherwise you have automated the mistakes and added a signature line.
